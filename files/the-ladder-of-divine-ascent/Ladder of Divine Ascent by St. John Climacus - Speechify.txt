The Ladder of Divine Ascent
By Saint John Climacus
The Ladder of Divine Ascent was written in the seventh century by a monk named John, abbot of the monastery on Mount Sinai, and known ever since by the name the book gave him: John Climacus, John of the Ladder. It was written at the request of another abbot, also named John, who led a monastery at Raithu on the Red Sea coast, and who asked for a rule of life he could hand to his monks. That request is the entire occasion of the book. It was never written for a general audience. It was written by a monk, for monks, at the request of a monk, and it assumes a reader who has already left the world behind and placed himself under the authority of a superior.
This book has become one of the most read spiritual texts in the Christian East, and it is read now by people who are not monks and never will be. We are not going to smooth it out for them. What follows presents John as John, in his own terms, with his own severity intact. He believed things about the body, about food, about tears, about weeping over one's sins, and about the shortness of a human life that most modern readers do not believe. He will say them plainly here. You can weigh them yourself.
The question the book is built around is this. What is the actual, ordered sequence of interior change that carries a person from a life ruled by the passions to a life ruled by love? Not what should a good life look like from a distance, but what happens first, and what happens after that, and what cannot happen until something else has happened. John's answer takes the form of a ladder. He borrowed the image from the Book of Genesis, where the patriarch Jacob sleeps at Bethel and sees a ladder set on the earth with its top reaching heaven, and angels ascending and descending on it. John's ladder has thirty rungs, one for each year of Christ's hidden life before his baptism, and each rung is a single subject: a thing to leave, a vice to fight, a virtue to acquire, or a state to be given.
The rungs are ordered on purpose. This is the whole architecture of the book, and it is what makes it different from a collection of spiritual advice. John claims that the vices breed one another in a chain and that the virtues do the same, so that you cannot skip. Obedience produces humility. Humility produces discernment. Discernment produces insight, and insight produces foresight. Anger fathers resentment, resentment fathers slander, slander needs talkativeness as its door, and talkativeness gives birth to despondency. If the order is real, then a person who attacks his pride directly, while leaving his stomach and his tongue alone, is wasting his effort. John says as much repeatedly. No one, he writes, climbs a ladder in a single stride.
We will work through the thirty rungs in five movements. The first is the break with the world. The second is the strange, central discovery of the book, which John calls joy-grief. The third is the long war against the obvious passions. The fourth is the war against the hidden ones, which John considers far more dangerous. The fifth is the summit, where the effort of the climber gives out and what remains has to be given rather than won.
Before We Begin: Six Words You Will Need
A short orientation first. John writes in a technical vocabulary that his original readers already knew, and if you do not know it, some of his most important sentences will slide past. This section is an addition by this companion, not part of the book. Six words.
The Passions
When John says passions, he does not mean strong feelings, and he does not mean enthusiasm. He means the settled, habitual disorders of a soul: anger as a standing disposition, greed as a permanent tilt, lust as an established groove. In Step Twenty-Six he is explicit that these are not natural to us and that God did not create them. Nature gives the seed for childbearing, he says, and we have perverted it into fornication. Nature gives us anger so we can be angry at the serpent, and we have turned it on our neighbour. Nature gives a desire for glory, and it was meant for the glory on high. So a passion, in John's usage, is a good faculty bent out of its proper direction and hardened there by habit. That is why he thinks it can be straightened.
The Eight Capital Vices
John inherits a list, worked out by earlier desert writers and passed to the Latin West by John Cassian, of eight generic vices from which the rest descend: gluttony, lust, avarice, sorrow, anger, despondency, vainglory, and pride. Three of these are treated as the root trio, which John names as gluttony, avarice, and vainglory, or in another arrangement as love of pleasure, love of money, and love of glory. He tells us in Step Twenty-Six that when he asked the elders which vice fathers which, they told him something he found unsatisfying and true at once: the irrational passions have no order and no reason, but only every sort of disorder and every kind of chaos. So he gives us the genealogy where he can and refuses to invent it where he cannot.
Dispassion
This is the single most misunderstood word in the book. The Greek is apatheia, and it does not mean apathy, indifference, or numbness. The translator is emphatic about this, and so is John. Dispassion, in the ecclesiastical sense, means freedom from passion through being filled with the Holy Spirit as a fruit of divine love. It is a state in which a burning love for God and for other people leaves no room left over for the selfish and animal passions. John can speak, without contradiction, of the fire of dispassion. It is not the cooling of desire. It is desire finally pointed at the one object large enough for it. Step Twenty-Nine is devoted to it, under the title heaven on earth.
Stillness
The Greek is hesychia, and the translation renders it sometimes as solitude, sometimes as stillness or quiet. It names both an outward condition, living alone in a cell rather than in a community, and an inward one, a mind that has stopped churning. The word gave its name to hesychasm, the whole tradition of contemplative prayer in the Christian East. Step Twenty-Seven is about it, and John's central warning there is that the outward version without the inward version is a disaster: a man who is sick in soul from some passion and attempts stillness, he says, is like a man who has jumped off a ship in open water and expects to reach shore on a plank.
Joy-Grief
The Greek word is charmolype, and it is John's own signature. It is a compound of joy and sorrow, and it names the specific quality of genuine repentance, which he insists is not misery. Step Seven has the title On mourning which causes joy, and in it John says he is amazed that a thing called grief should contain gladness woven into it like honey in a comb. He calls it a pleasureless pleasure. Everything in the book from Step Five to Step Seven depends on the reader accepting that mourning and gladness are not opposites, and that a man crying over his own sins may be, at that exact moment, the happiest man in the monastery.
The Frame
Last, the setting. The he in this book is a monk. He has taken vows, he lives in a monastery or a cell in the desert, he owns nothing, he has a superior whose word he obeys without argument, and he expects to die in the place he entered. When John gives instruction about food, sleep, women, money, travel, or speech, he is regulating that life, not offering general advice. There is exactly one passage where he speaks to people in the world, in Step One, and it is instructive how short it is. Some married people living in the world asked him how they could possibly lead the solitary life, and his answer was a brief list: do all the good you can, speak evil of no one, steal from no one, lie to no one, be arrogant to no one, hate no one, go to church, be compassionate to the needy, offend no one, wreck no one's marriage, and be content with your own wife. If you behave like that, he told them, you will not be far from the Kingdom of Heaven. That is the whole of his counsel for laypeople. Everything else in the thirty rungs is addressed to the monastery.
Movement One: Cutting the Rope
The first four rungs are about departure. John treats the break with ordinary life as a technical operation with stages, and he is interested in a question that a modern reader might not think to ask: not whether you left, but why, because he thinks the motive determines whether the departure holds.
Step One: Renunciation, and the Three Motives
John opens the book by sorting all rational beings by their relation to God, from the bodiless intelligences that surround him, through his true servants, down through the worthless servants who were baptized and did not keep their vows, to those estranged from him, and finally to his active enemies, who not only reject the commandment themselves but make war on those who keep it. He then narrows to definitions. A Christian is one who imitates Christ in thought, word, and deed as far as a human being can. A lover of God is one who lives in communion with everything natural and sinless and neglects no good within reach. A monk is one who, inside an earthly and soiled body, labours toward the rank and condition of the bodiless beings. And withdrawal from the world, he says, is a voluntary hatred of material things that people boast of, and a denial of nature in order to attain what is above nature.
Then comes the passage the book turns on. Everyone who has willingly left the world, John says, has done so for one of three reasons: for the sake of the coming Kingdom, or because of the multitude of his sins, or for love of God. If none of the three moved him, his departure was unreasonable. And these three motives do not hold equally well. The man who renounces the world out of fear, John says, is like burning incense, which begins in fragrance and ends in smoke. The man who leaves in hope of reward is like a millstone, which turns forever in the same place and goes nowhere, revolving around itself. But the man who withdraws out of love for God has obtained fire at the very outset, and like fire set to fuel it soon kindles a larger fire.
The reason this matters is that John is describing the failure mode of every serious undertaking. Fear starts hot and cools. Reward-seeking sustains motion without producing movement. Only the third motive is self-amplifying. And he adds, with characteristic realism, that fervour is losable and recoverable by one specific route. When the soul betrays itself and loses that longed-for fervour, he says, let it carefully investigate the reason it was lost and arm itself against whatever caused it, because the former fervour can only return through the same door by which it left.
He is also, in this first step, remarkably unwilling to disqualify anybody. Do not condemn a renunciation that happened by accident or by mere circumstance, he says, and gives three examples of unintended good outcomes: exiles who met the emperor by chance on the road and ended up dining in his palace, seed that fell casually on the ground and bore abundant fruit, and a man who came to a hospital for some other reason entirely and was so overcome by the physician's kindness that he stayed for treatment and lost the darkness on his eyes. For some people, John says, the unintentional turned out stronger and surer than the deliberate intention of others. And let nobody plead his sins as a reason he is unworthy of the vow. Where there is much corruption, considerable treatment is needed. The healthy do not go to a hospital.
Step Two: Detachment, and the Transplanted Plants
The second rung is about the interior version of the same break. You can leave a place and carry it with you. John's test for whether an attachment is really gone is unsentimental: if anyone thinks he is unattached to some object but is grieved at its loss, he is completely deceiving himself. He also identifies a temptation that arrives immediately after the departure, in which the demons suggest to the new monk that he should envy the merciful and compassionate people still living in the world, and feel sorry for himself as being cut off from the chance to do good. The aim of that suggestion, John says, is either to send him back or, if he stays, to sink him in despair.
The most useful image in this step is the one about plants. John says he has watched people who lived austere lives in the world, keeping vigils and fasts and hardships, enter the monastery and stop doing any of it. He wondered why, and concluded this. In the world they had planted many different plants of the virtues, and those plants were watered by vainglory as though by an underground sewage pipe, hoed by ostentation, and manured with praise. Transplanted to desert soil, where nobody was watching and there was no foul water to feed them, they withered at once. Water-loving plants do not bear fruit in a hard and arid training field. It is a merciless observation about hidden motives, and it explains a phenomenon everyone has witnessed: the disciplined person whose discipline evaporates the moment there is no audience.
This step also contains John's practical definition of the narrow way, which he offers because he thinks people routinely mistake the broad way for the narrow one. The narrow way, he says, means mortification of the stomach, standing all night, water in moderation, short rations of bread, and then the purifying draught of dishonour: sneers, derision, insults, the cutting out of one's own will, patience under annoyance, unmurmuring endurance of scorn, disregard of insults, and the habit, when wronged, of bearing it stoutly, when slandered of not being indignant, when humiliated of not being angry, and when condemned of being humble. Notice that more than half the list is about being treated badly by other people rather than about diet or sleep. That proportion is deliberate and it recurs through the whole book.
And he specifies that there are three renunciations, not one. The first is of business, people, and parents. The second is the cutting out of one's own will. The third, and the one nobody expects, is renunciation of the conceit that grows out of obedience itself, the quiet pride a man takes in how well he submits.
Step Three: Exile, and the Warning About Dreams
The third rung is exile, or pilgrimage: the deliberate departure from one's own country and people. John's definition is dense and worth hearing in full, because it is a portrait of the interior condition he is after. Exile, he says, means modest manners, wisdom that remains unknown, prudence that most people do not recognize as such, a hidden life, an invisible intention, unseen meditation, a desire for humiliation, a longing for hardship, a constant determination to love God, an abundance of charity, the renunciation of vainglory, and depth of silence.
Then the qualification, which is typical of him: great and praiseworthy as this is, it requires great discretion, because not every kind of exile carried to extremes is good. And the specific danger he names is that exile itself becomes a source of vainglory. If every prophet goes unhonoured in his own country, he says, then beware lest your exile become an occasion for pride. He tells the reader to hide his noble birth and not to glory in his distinction, and he identifies with precision the thought that arrives after a year or two away: that one has now acquired some piety and continence, and ought to go home for the edification of many, as an example, for the profit of those who saw one's former loose life. And if one happens to be eloquent and to have picked up some learning, then the thought becomes that one could be a saviour of souls and a teacher in the world. That, John says, is how a man wastes at sea what he gathered safely in harbour. Imitate not Lot's wife but Lot, and do not look back.
The reason a modern reader should attend to this step is the passage on dreams appended to it, which is one of the clearest pieces of practical psychology in the book. John says he is inserting it here and dogmatizing about nothing. When we leave home and family for God's sake, he says, the demons try to disturb us with dreams that show our relatives grieving, or dying, or destitute and captive on our account. And a man who believes in dreams is like a person running after his own shadow and trying to catch it.
He then explains why some dreams appear to come true. The demons of vainglory prophesy in dreams. Being unscrupulous, they guess the future and foretell it, and when a guess lands we are amazed, and elated, and start to believe we are approaching the gift of foreknowledge. A demon, John says, is often a prophet to whoever believes him, and always a liar to whoever despises him. He can see what is happening in the lower air, so he notices that someone is dying and reports it in a dream to the credulous, but he knows nothing about the future by foreknowledge, because if he did, sorcerers would be able to predict our deaths, and they cannot. John's rule for sorting dreams is severe and simple: he who believes in dreams is completely inexperienced, and he who distrusts all dreams is a wise man. Believe only those dreams that foretell judgment and torment for you, and if despair afflicts you, distrust even those.
Step Four: Obedience, the Longest Rung
Step Four is far longer than any other step in the book, and it is the one that carries John's field reporting. He had visited a great monastery near Alexandria, stayed a considerable time, watched everything, asked questions of everyone, and he writes it down here as case material. Before the stories, though, come the definitions, and they are as hard as anything in the book.
Obedience, John says, is absolute renunciation of our own life, clearly expressed in bodily action. It is the mortification of the limbs while the mind stays alive. It is unquestioning movement, voluntary death, simple life, carefree danger, spontaneous defence by God, fearlessness of death, a safe voyage, a sleeper's progress. It is the tomb of the will and the resurrection of humility. And then the image everything else hangs from: a corpse does not argue or reason about what is good or what seems bad, because the one who has devoutly put the novice's soul to death will answer for everything. Obedience, in his final formulation, is the abandonment of discernment in a wealth of discernment.
John is aware that this is dangerous and he builds in the safeguard himself, at the front. Before you enter this life, he says, if there is any vice or pride in you, you ought first to question and examine and even test your helmsman, so as not to mistake a sailor for the pilot, a sick man for a doctor, a passionate man for a dispassionate one, or the open sea for a harbour, and so wreck your own soul in a hurry. Test him first. But once you have entered the arena of obedience, stop judging him, even if you notice slight failings, because he is only a man. He is even specific about matching the physician to the disease: if you are prone to lust, do not choose as your trainer a wonderworker who greets everyone with a welcome and a meal, but an ascetic who will hear of no consolation in food. If you are haughty, let him be stern and unyielding rather than kindly. Do not go looking for elders with the gift of foreknowledge, but for ones who are humble and whose character and manner of living correspond to your particular sickness.
The Robber at the Door
Now the stories. The first is the one John says he never forgot. A robber applied for admission to that monastery, and the abbot gave him a week of complete rest just to observe the life. At the end of the week he asked him privately whether he wanted to stay, and seeing that the man sincerely did, asked him what evil he had done in the world. The man confessed readily. So the abbot said: I want you to tell this in front of all the brethren. And because the man really did hate his sin, he agreed without hesitating, and added that he would say it in the middle of the city of Alexandria if that was wanted.
So on a Sunday, after the reading of the Gospel, the abbot gathered all two hundred and thirty monks in the church. The man was brought in with his hands tied behind his back, dressed in a hair shirt, his head sprinkled with ashes, dragged forward by several of the brothers who struck him moderately as he came. Nobody knew what was happening and a wail went up. And when the man reached the doors of the church, the abbot called out from the sanctuary in a loud voice: Stop. You are not worthy to enter here.
The man later swore to John that he had not heard a human voice but thunder. He fell on his face, shaking all over, and lay there wetting the floor with his tears while the abbot, in front of everyone, told him to give the details. And with terror he confessed his sins one after another, sins of the flesh, natural and unnatural, with human beings and with animals, poisoning, murder, and much more that John says is indecent even to write down. When he had finished, the abbot immediately had him clothed as a monk and numbered among the brethren.
John asked him afterwards, privately, why he had staged such an extraordinary spectacle. The abbot gave two reasons. First, to deliver the penitent from future shame by means of present shame, and it worked, he said, because the man did not get up off the floor until he had received remission of all his sins. One of the brothers who had been present told the abbot afterwards that he had seen someone terrible standing there holding a pen and a writing tablet, and that as the prostrate man named each sin, the figure crossed it out. Second, the abbot said, because there are others in this brotherhood carrying unconfessed sins, and I want to induce them to confess too, since without that nobody obtains forgiveness.
Whatever a reader makes of the vision of the figure with the pen, the mechanism the abbot describes is worth holding onto, because John states it directly in the same step: by resolving to make one's confession, the soul is held back from sinning as if by a bridle, since what we do not confess we do fearlessly, as though in the dark. And he warns against the sophisticated evasion. Do not be deceived by the spirit of conceit into confessing your own sins as though they belonged to somebody else. You cannot escape shame except by shame. It is the habit of the demons to persuade us either not to confess at all, or to confess as though the sins were another person's, or to lay the blame on someone else. Lay bare your wound to the physician and say: it is my wound, Father, my plague, caused by my own negligence and nothing else. No one is to blame, no man, no spirit, no body, nothing but my own carelessness.
What John Saw in That Monastery
The rest of his report is a portrait of a community, and it is one of the most attractive passages in the book. They were bound to each other, he says, by an indissoluble bond of love, and what struck him as more wonderful still, it was free of familiarity and idle talk. Above everything else they tried never to wound a brother's conscience. If anyone showed hatred to another he was sent to the isolation house like a convict. When one monk spoke ill of his neighbour to the abbot, the abbot had him driven out at once, saying that he could not permit a visible devil in the monastery as well as an invisible one.
They policed each other gently and without words. If someone began speaking abusively or criticizing people or simply talking idly while the superior was absent, another brother would remind him with a secret nod and quietly stop it. If the man did not notice the nod, the one who gave it would make a prostration and withdraw. When one of them made a mistake, the others would ask the abbot for permission to take on the punishment themselves, which is why that abbot, once he learned they were doing this, began giving lighter penances, knowing the person being punished was probably innocent, and stopped asking who had actually made the error.
John asked several of the old men what they had gotten out of decades of this. He found elders who had spent fifty years in obedience. Some told him they had attained a deep humility with which they permanently repelled every assault. Others said they had obtained complete freedom from pain under insult and slander. He describes the sight of white-haired elders of holy beauty running about in obedience like children and taking great delight in their own humiliation, and he calls it an awful and angelic spectacle.
Two small details from that community are worth keeping. The first is the baker. John noticed that this man had attained constant recollection and constant tears while he worked, and pressed him to explain how. The baker said: I have never thought I was serving men, but God. And having judged myself unworthy of all rest, by this visible fire I am unceasingly reminded of the future flame. The visible fire was the bakehouse oven. The second is the notebook. John observed that the brother in charge of the refectory always carried a small book tucked in his belt, and learned that he wrote his thoughts in it each day and showed them all to the abbot. And not only that man. Very many of the brothers were doing the same, on the abbot's orders.
Isidore, Laurence, Abbacyrus
Then the individual cases, and John gives us their names. Isidore was a man of magistrate's rank from Alexandria who had recently entered. The abbot found him full of mischief, cruel, sly, fierce, and arrogant, and said to him: if you have decided to take on the yoke of Christ, I want you first of all to learn obedience. Isidore answered: as iron to the smith, so I surrender myself to you. So the abbot took the comparison and put the iron to work. He stationed Isidore at the monastery gate and told him to prostrate himself to everyone going in and out. He stood there for seven years.
John asked him, while he was still alive, what his mind had been occupied with all that time at the gate. Isidore said: in the beginning I judged that I had been sold into slavery for my sins, and so I made the prostration with bitterness, with great effort, and as it were with blood. After a year my heart no longer felt sorrow, and I began to expect a reward for my obedience from God himself. But when another year had gone by, I began to be deeply conscious that I was unworthy even to live in the monastery, or to see and meet the fathers, or to receive the Mysteries. And I did not dare look anyone in the face, but bent low with my eyes and lower still with my thought, and sincerely asked for the prayers of everyone coming in and going out. Note the direction of travel across those three stages: from resentment, to expectation of reward, to something that no longer thinks in terms of transaction at all.
After the seven years the abbot judged him fully ready to be professed and ordained. Isidore begged, repeatedly and through intermediaries including John, to be allowed to finish as he was, hinting that his death was near. It was. Ten days after the abbot let him stay at the gate, he died. He had told the porter of the monastery that if he had found favour with God, the porter would shortly be joined to him. The porter died seven days later.
Laurence is a smaller and stranger story. Once, at table, the abbot leaned over and put his mouth to John's ear and asked whether he would like to be shown divine prudence in extreme old age. Then he called forward a monk named Laurence, who had been in the community forty-eight years and was second priest of the monastery. Laurence came, prostrated, took the blessing, and stood up. And the abbot said nothing whatsoever to him, but simply left him standing there beside the table, not eating, for an hour or perhaps two, while the meal went on. John writes that he was ashamed to look the old man in the face, because his hair was completely white and he was eighty years old.
Afterwards John asked him what he had been thinking about while he stood there. Laurence said: I thought of the shepherd as the image of Christ, and I considered that I had not received the command from him at all but from God. And so I stood praying, Father John, not as before a table of men but as before the altar of God, and because of my faith and love for the shepherd, no evil thought about him entered my mind, for love does not resent an injury. And know this, Father: if anyone surrenders himself to simplicity and voluntary innocence, he no longer gives the devil either time or place to attack him.
Abbacyrus had been there fifteen years, and John noticed that almost everyone mistreated him and that the servers drove him out of the refectory nearly every day, because he was by nature a little too talkative. John asked him why he put up with going to bed hungry so often. Abbacyrus said: believe me, Father, my fathers are testing me to see whether I am really a monk, and they are not doing it in earnest. Knowing what the abbot intends, and what they intend, I bear it without getting depressed, and I have done so now for fifteen years. When I entered they told me that those who renounce the world are tested for thirty years. And rightly, Father John, because gold is not purified without trial. He lived two more years and then died, and his last words to the fathers were: I am thankful, thankful, to the Lord and to you. Having been tempted by you for my salvation, I have lived seventeen years without temptations from devils.
Macedonius, and the Property of Men
Macedonius was the chief deacon. Two days before the feast of Theophany he asked leave to go into Alexandria on a personal errand, promising to be back in time for the festival, and the abbot released him. He returned a day late. The abbot deposed him from the diaconate and put him among the lowest novices, and Macedonius accepted the sentence as calmly as if it had been passed on somebody else. After forty days the abbot restored him to his rank. Barely a day later Macedonius asked to be put back down among the novices, claiming that he had committed an unforgivable sin in the city. The abbot knew this was untrue and that the man was inventing a sin in order to be humiliated, and he gave him what he asked for. So there was an honoured elder with white hair living as a novice and sincerely begging everyone to pray for him, saying: I fell into the fornication of disobedience.
John got him alone and asked why. Macedonius said: never have I felt such relief from every conflict, and such sweetness of divine light, as now. And then he said the sentence that is probably the most quoted line in the entire book, and it is worth hearing exactly. It is the property of angels not to fall, and even, as some say, quite impossible for them to fall. It is the property of men to fall, and to rise again as often as this may happen. But it is the property of devils, and devils alone, not to rise once they have fallen.
That sentence is the hinge of John's whole treatment of failure, and it is why the book, for all its severity, is not a counsel of despair. Falling is not the diagnostic. Staying down is. Elsewhere in the same step he says it directly: do not be surprised that you fall every day, do not give up, but stand your ground courageously, and the angel who guards you will honour your patience. And he adds the physician's note. While a wound is fresh and warm it is easy to heal, but old, neglected, festering ones are hard, and require much cutting and plastering and cauterizing, and many become incurable from long neglect.
Menas, and the Two Ways of Fighting
Menas held the second place after the abbot and had been in the community fifty-nine years, filling every office in turn. He died a week before John left. Three days after his death, when the customary rites had been performed, the whole place where he lay filled with fragrance, and the abbot permitted them to open the coffin. John says they all saw fragrant myrrh flowing like two fountains from the dead man's feet, and the abbot said to them: look, the sweat of his toils and labours has been offered to God as myrrh, and truly accepted.
One story about Menas from the other fathers. The abbot had wanted to test his patience. Menas came to his cell in the evening, prostrated as usual, and asked for a word of instruction. The abbot left him lying on the ground until the hour of the Office, then finally blessed him, rebuked him for being fond of self-display and impatient, and told him to get up. He knew Menas would bear it, and he staged the scene for everyone else's benefit. A disciple of Menas admitted he had been curious whether the old man had fallen asleep down there on the floor, and Menas assured him that while he lay there he had recited the entire psalter from memory.
John closes his account of that community with a conversation he clearly enjoyed. He had started a discussion on silence with some of the most experienced elders, and they answered him smiling, in a jovial mood and in a friendly way: we, Father John, being material, live a material life. We prefer to wage war according to the measure of our own weakness, and we consider it better to struggle with men, who are sometimes fierce and sometimes penitent, than with demons, who are continually raging and up in arms against us. That is a whole theory of the community life delivered as a joke, and John was persuaded by it.
One of them then gave him the fullest description of the communal path in the book, and it is a good place to hear what John thinks obedience actually feels like from inside. Gird your loins with the towel of obedience. Rise from the supper of silence and wash the brethren's feet in a spirit of contrition, or rather, roll yourself under the feet of the community in spiritual self-abasement. Place strict and unsleeping guards at the gate of your heart. Control your wandering mind in your distracted body. In the middle of all the actions and movements of your limbs, practise inner stillness. And, most paradoxical of all, in the middle of commotion be unmoved in soul. Curb your tongue, which rages to leap into arguments; seventy times seven in a day, wrestle with that tyrant. Fix your mind to your soul as to the wood of a cross, to be struck like an anvil with blow after blow of the hammers, to be mocked, abused, ridiculed and wronged, without being in the least crushed or broken, but staying quite calm and immovable. Shed your own will like a garment of shame, and stripped of it, enter the practice ground.
Acacius, and the Elder Who Committed Murder
Two more cases, both told to John by a monk called John the Sabbaite, and both about obedience under a bad superior, which is the hardest question this step raises.
In a monastery in Asia there was an elder who was extremely careless and undisciplined. John the Sabbaite says he mentions this not to pass judgment but simply to state the fact. Somehow this elder acquired a disciple, a young man named Acacius, simple-hearted but prudent in thought. And the elder tormented him daily, not only with insults and indignities but with blows, for nine years. John the Sabbaite would meet the young man and ask how he was, and Acacius would show him a black eye, or a scarred neck, or a wound on his head. And knowing that he was a worker, John the Sabbaite would say: well done, well done, endure, and it will be for your good. He is careful to add that the young man's patience was not senseless endurance.
After nine years Acacius died. Five days after the burial, the elder went to another old man living there and said: Father, Brother Acacius is dead. And the old man said: believe me, elder, I do not believe it. Come and see, said the other. So they went to the cemetery, and the old man called out to the dead man as to someone alive: are you dead, Brother Acacius? And, the story says, the good doer of obedience answered from the grave: how is it possible, Father, for a man who is a doer of obedience to die? At which the elder who had been his master fell on his face in tears, and afterwards asked for a cell near the tomb and lived in it devoutly, telling the fathers ever after: I have committed murder.
The second case is John the Sabbaite's own, though he told it to John Climacus as though it were about another man, under the name Antiochus, and John says he later found out for certain that it was himself. He had left a gentle master who was becoming too fond of him, judging that this would be fatal, and settled in a community in Pontus with a letter of introduction. His first night there he dreamed that his account was being drawn up, and when the reckoning was finished he was left owing a hundred pounds of gold. He woke and said to himself: poor Antiochus, you fall a long way short of your debt.
He lived there three years in unquestioning obedience, held in contempt by everyone and insulted as the foreigner, since he was the only outsider in the house. Then he dreamed again, and this time someone handed him a credit note for ten pounds against the debt. He woke and said: still only ten. When will I pay the rest? And then: poor Antiochus, still more toil and dishonour for you. So from that point on he began to pretend to be a blockhead, without ever neglecting to serve anyone, and the fathers, seeing that he willingly served in that condition, gave him every heavy job in the monastery. He lived like that for thirteen years, and then dreamed of the same figures giving him a receipt in full settlement. And whenever the community imposed on him after that, he says, he remembered his debt and bore it courageously.
That is a strange and instructive story, because the accounting is entirely his own invention or entirely a gift, depending on how you read the dreams, and either way the effect is the same: he found a way to interpret thirteen years of humiliation as payment on a debt he had already accepted, and so the humiliation stopped being an injustice. John records without comment that the dream arithmetic worked.
And John does not leave the question of the bad superior comfortable. Later in the same step, the same John the Sabbaite gives a rule to a young monk who asks the obvious question: what if my trainer lives a lax life? The elder answers: even if you see him committing fornication, do not leave him, but say to yourself, friend, why are you here? Then you will see all pride vanish from you, and lust wither. Modern readers will find that appalling and should not be told otherwise. It is what the text says, in a world where the alternative on offer was a young monk alone in the desert with his own judgment, which John regarded as more dangerous still. He is consistent about the reasoning even where the conclusion is hard: it is dangerous for an inexperienced soldier to leave his regiment and fight alone, and a man who takes away a blind man's guide, a flock's shepherd, a child's father, a patient's doctor, or a ship's pilot, endangers everything.
The Two Snares
John ends Step Four by naming the two specific ways obedience fails, and he calls them snares.
The first snare is discouragement. The devil attacks those under obedience by making them dry and barren, sluggish in prayer, drowsy, confused, and darkened, in order to tear them out of the struggle by convincing them that they have gained nothing from all their obedience and are only sliding backwards. And he does not give them time to reflect, John says, that the providential withdrawal of what we imagined were blessings is often exactly what leads us into the deepest humility.
The second snare is more interesting, because it disguises itself as progress. John says he has seen people who, under a good director, became filled with compunction, meek, temperate, zealous, free of inner conflict, and fervent. And then the demons came and sowed the thought that they were now qualified for the solitary life, and that in solitude they would attain freedom from the passions as a final prize. Thus deceived, John says, they left the harbour and put out to sea, and when a storm came down on them they were pitifully endangered on that foul and bitter ocean, because they had no pilots. The trap is not that solitude is bad. It is that the very first fruits of obedience feel like a graduation certificate, and are not one. The devil suggests impossible virtues to those under obedience, John says, and unsuitable ideas to those in solitude. Scan the mind of an inexperienced novice and you will find it crowded with desires: for quiet, for the strictest fast, for uninterrupted prayer, for absolute freedom from vanity, for unbroken remembrance of death, for continual compunction, for perfect freedom from anger, for deep silence, for surpassing purity. And if by God's providence he does not have these things at the start, he rushes off to another life and is deceived.
The step closes with the chain that governs the rest of the book. From obedience comes humility. From humility comes discernment, as Cassian said. From discernment comes insight, and from insight, foresight. And who, John asks, would not follow this fair way of obedience, seeing such blessings in store for him?
Movement Two: The Grief That Gladdens
Three rungs sit at the centre of the book's foundation: repentance, the remembrance of death, and mourning. They are the hardest material in the Ladder for a modern reader, and they are also where John says something genuinely unexpected. If you take away only one thing from this movement, take away the compound word: joy-grief.
Step Five: The Prison
John begins with definitions, and they are unusually good. Repentance, he says, is the renewal of baptism. It is a contract with God for a second life. A penitent is a buyer of humility. Repentance is a constant distrust of bodily comfort, self-condemning reflection, and carefree self-care. It is the daughter of hope and the renunciation of despair. A penitent is an undisgraced convict. Repentance is reconciliation with the Lord by the practice of good deeds contrary to the sins.
Notice two of those. The daughter of hope and the renunciation of despair: John's repentance is structurally the opposite of self-hatred, because self-hatred has already given up. And reconciliation by the practice of good deeds contrary to the sins: repentance for him is not primarily a feeling but a reversal in behaviour, a deliberate practice of the opposite.
Then he tells us about the place he calls the Prison. About a mile from the great monastery there was a separate establishment for monks who had fallen into serious sin after entering the brotherhood. It had no comfort of any kind. No smoke, no wine, no oil in the food, nothing but bread and light vegetables. The abbot shut men in there, each in a separate cell or at most in pairs, and kept them until he had assurance that each had amended. A great man named Isaac was the sub-prior, and he required of them almost unceasing prayer, and to prevent despondency they were given a large quantity of palm leaves to weave into baskets. John asked permission to visit. He stayed thirty days.
What he describes is extreme, and it should be heard as he wrote it. He saw men standing in the open air all night until morning without moving their feet, dazed with sleep by force of nature, allowing themselves no rest, reproaching themselves and driving sleep off with insults. Men standing in prayer with their hands tied behind their backs like criminals, faces darkened by sorrow and bent to the earth, considering themselves unworthy to look up. Men sitting on the ground in sackcloth and ashes with their faces hidden between their knees, striking the earth with their foreheads. Men beating their breasts continually. Some watered the ground with tears; others, unable to weep, struck themselves. Some lamented over their own souls as over the dead. From the number of their prostrations, John says, their knees seemed to have turned to wood. Their eyes were sunk deep into their sockets. They had no hair. Their cheeks were scalded and bruised by hot tears. Their breasts were livid from blows, and from the beating of the chest they spat blood.
Their prayers, as he records them, are almost entirely without confidence and almost entirely without despair, which is a difficult combination to hold. Open to us, Judge, open. We have shut ourselves out by our sins. Some said only: have mercy, have mercy. Others, more plaintively: forgive, Lord, forgive if it is possible. And some, out of unspeakable humility, condemned themselves as unworthy of forgiveness and cried out that it was not in their power to justify themselves before God. Some begged to be punished here and receive mercy in the next world. Others said: spare us from future punishment even though we are not worthy of the Kingdom, and that will satisfy us.
They spoke to each other, John says, in a kind of mutual interrogation: are we accomplishing anything, brothers? Are we obtaining our requests? Will the Lord accept us again? Will he open to us? And the answer they gave one another is the most human moment in the chapter. Who knows, as our brothers the Ninevites said, whether God will relent. In any case, let us do our part. If he opens the door, well and good. And if not, blessed is the Lord God who in his justice has closed the door to us. At least let us keep knocking until the end of our lives. Perhaps he will open to us for our persistence.
The death scenes in the Prison are the part John clearly could not shake. When the others learned that one of them was dying, they gathered around while his mind was still working and asked him, with tears and with a tenderness John records carefully, a series of questions. How are you, brother and fellow criminal? What will you say? What do you hope? Have you accomplished what you sought with such labour, or not? Has the door been opened to you, or are you still under judgment? Have you received any sort of assurance, or is your hope still uncertain? Has any inner voice said to you, behold, you are made whole, or, your sins are forgiven you? Tell us, we beg you, so that we too may know what state we shall be in, because your time is closed now and you will never have another opportunity. Some of the dying answered with a verse: blessed is God, who has not turned away my prayer, nor his mercy from me. Others said, more doubtfully: will our soul pass through the impassable water? And some answered most sorrowfully of all: woe to the soul that has not kept its vow intact. In this hour, and only in this hour, it will know what is prepared for it.
John says that watching this, he nearly despaired of himself, comparing his own indifference to their suffering. And then, oddly, he says something else: I found so much pleasure in their grief that I forgot myself and could not contain myself. When he came back to the great monastery after thirty days, the abbot looked at him and saw that he was completely changed, and asked whether he had seen the struggles of those who labour at their task. John's answer is his own verdict on the Prison, and it is the reason the chapter is in the book: I saw them, Father, and I was amazed. And I consider those fallen mourners more blessed than those who have not fallen and are not mourning over themselves, because as a result of their fall they have risen by a sure resurrection.
John himself flags that this chapter is dangerous, and he flags it precisely. I am fully aware, my good friends, that the struggles I have described will seem to some incredible, to others hard to believe, and to some will seem to breed despair. To a courageous soul they will serve as a spur and a shaft of fire. A man who is not up to this will recognize his own weakness and, easily obtaining humility through self-reproach, will run after the first man, and I do not know whether he may not even overtake him. But the careless man should leave my stories alone, in case he despairs and squanders even the little he has accomplished. That is an author telling part of his audience not to read his own chapter, and it should be taken at face value.
The most useful things in Step Five are the sentences that generalize. It is impossible for those of us who have fallen into the pit of our own iniquities ever to be drawn out of it, John says, unless we sink into the abyss of the humility of the penitents. Nothing equals or excels God's mercies, therefore he who despairs is committing suicide. A sign of true repentance is the acknowledgement that we deserve all the troubles, visible and invisible, that come to us, and even greater ones. And a sign of deliverance from our falls is the continual acknowledgment of our indebtedness. He also gives one warning about false peace of mind: we must consider carefully whether our conscience has stopped accusing us not because we are pure, but because it is submerged in evil.
He notices something else about recovery that is easy to miss. We do not return by the road we went astray on, he says, but by another, shorter route. He does not explain what he means, and it is better not to over-explain it. The observation stands on its own: the way back is not the way out, retraced.
And one story, which he uses to prove that this is not a mechanism only for extreme cases. The abbot told him that about ten years earlier there had been a brother in the community who was extremely zealous and active, and that seeing him burning so hot, the abbot had trembled for him, in case the devil out of envy should trip his foot against a stone as he sped along, which is what happens to people who walk fast. And that is exactly what happened. Late one evening the man came to the abbot, showed him the open wound, and asked for cauterization. When he saw that his physician did not want to make too severe an incision, because he deserved sympathy, he threw himself on the ground, embraced the abbot's feet, wet them with tears, and begged to be shut in the Prison. It is impossible for me not to go there, he said. And he urged his doctor to change kindness into severity, which John notes is a rare and unusual thing for a sick man to do. He went to the penitents, became their companion, and on the eighth day he died. He had asked not to be buried, but the abbot buried him among the fathers, because after his week of slavery he was released on the eighth day as a free man.
Step Six: Remembrance of Death
Step Six is short and it is the engine underneath everything in the book about food, sleep, and time. The remembrance of death, John says, is a daily death, and the remembrance of our departure is an hourly sighing. As bread is the most necessary of all foods, the thought of death is the most necessary of all works.
He immediately distinguishes it from morbidity. There is a natural fear of death, which comes from our disobedience, and there is terror at death, which he says is evidence of unrepented sin. And he makes a distinction between two states: he who daily expects death with undoubting trust is virtuous, but he who hourly yields himself to it is a saint. He also warns that not every desire for death is good. Some, sinning constantly out of habit, pray for death with humility. Some, refusing to repent, invoke death out of despair. Some, out of self-esteem, consider themselves already free of passion and for a while have no fear of death at all. And some, through the action of the Holy Spirit, genuinely long for their departure. Four wishes for the same thing, four completely different conditions.
He then answers a question people evidently asked him. If the remembrance of death is so useful, why has God hidden the hour of it from us? Because, John says, that hiddenness is itself how our salvation is accomplished. No one who knew the date of his own death would come to baptism or the monastic life at once. Everyone would spend all his days in wrongdoing and approach repentance only on the last day, and by then long habit would have confirmed him in vice and left him incorrigible.
He is honest that this remembrance cannot be manufactured. Let us rest assured that the remembrance of death, like every other blessing, is a gift of God, he says. How else is it that often when we are standing at the very tombs we are left tearless and hard, and frequently, with no such sight in front of us, we are full of compunction?
The story he tells here is Hesychius the Horebite, and it is the clearest illustration in the book of John's claim about the property of men. Hesychius had passed his life in complete negligence, paying no attention whatsoever to his soul. Then he became extremely ill, and for an hour he left his body. When he came to himself he begged everyone to leave him immediately. He built up the door of his cell, and he stayed inside for twelve years, never speaking a word to anyone, eating nothing but bread and water, motionless, so absorbed in whatever he had seen that he never changed his place and silently shed hot tears. When he was about to die they broke open the door and went in, and after many questions the only thing they got from him was this: forgive me. No one who has acquired the remembrance of death will ever be able to sin. They buried him near the fort, and some days later they went to look for his relics and could not find them. So by his true and praiseworthy repentance, John says, the Lord showed us that even after long negligence he accepts those who desire to amend.
Two practical lines from this step deserve to be carried out of it. A man who wants to keep the remembrance of death and judgment in front of him while also giving himself to material cares and distractions is like a man swimming who wants to clap his hands. And: it is impossible to spend the present day devoutly unless we regard it as the last of our whole life. John adds, with visible pleasure, that even the pagans said something of the sort, since they defined philosophy as meditation on death.
Step Seven: Mourning Which Causes Joy
Now the centre. Step Seven is about penthos, mourning, and the title tells you the argument: mourning which causes joy.
John's definition first. Mourning, according to God, is a sadness of soul and the disposition of a sorrowing heart, which madly seeks what it thirsts for, and when it fails in the search, pursues it painfully and follows in its wake, grieving. Or, he says, put it this way: mourning is a golden spur in a soul stripped of all attachment and all ties, fixed by holy sorrow to keep watch over the heart. A spur, not a weight. That distinction runs through the whole chapter.
He then makes the claim about tears that is his most audacious. Greater than baptism itself, he says, is the fountain of tears after baptism, though it is somewhat audacious to say so. Baptism washes away the evils that were in us before it; the sins committed after baptism are washed away by tears. And because we all received baptism as infants, we have all defiled it, and we cleanse it anew with tears. If God in his love for mankind had not given us tears, he says, few indeed and hard to find would be those in a state of grace. This is John's answer to the problem of the sinning Christian, and it is why the Ladder is a book about weeping rather than a book about rules.
He tracks progress in mourning by three stages, which is one of his standard moves. A characteristic of those still progressing in blessed mourning is temperance and silence of the lips. Of those who have made progress, freedom from anger and patient endurance of injuries. And of the perfect: humility, a thirst for dishonour, a voluntary craving for involuntary afflictions, non-condemnation of sinners, and compassion even beyond one's own strength. The first are acceptable, he says, the second praiseworthy, but blessed are those who hunger for hardship and thirst for dishonour, because they will have their fill of the food that does not cloy.
He is rigorous about false tears, and this is where the chapter earns its keep. Many of the Fathers say that the question of tears, particularly in beginners, is obscure and hard to make out, because tears are born in many different ways: from nature, from God, from adverse suffering, from praiseworthy suffering, from vainglory, from licentiousness, from love, from the remembrance of death, and from many other causes. So: do not trust your fountains of tears before your soul has been perfectly purified, because wine cannot be trusted when it is drawn straight from the vat. And a specific diagnostic: when we see anger and pride in people who seem to be mourning in a way pleasing to God, their tears are to be regarded as repugnant to God. Also this, which is very fine: he who in his heart is proud of his tears, and secretly condemns those who do not weep, is like a man who asks the king for a weapon against his enemy and then commits suicide with it.
He warns against the split life, and the image is memorable. He who sometimes mourns and sometimes indulges in luxury and laughter is like a man stoning the dog of sensuality with bread. In appearance he is driving it off. In fact he is teaching it to stay.
He also names a trap in the timing of compunction that anyone who has tried a discipline will recognize. If we watch carefully, he says, we shall often find a bitter joke played on us by the demons. When we are full, they stir us up to compunction, and when we are fasting they harden our hearts, so that deceived by spurious tears we give ourselves up to indulgence. We must not listen to them, but do the opposite. In other words, the sudden spiritual feeling that arrives exactly when discipline is not being kept is suspect, and the dryness that arrives exactly when it is being kept is normal.
And one aside that tells you a great deal about John's intellectual temperament. Theology, he says, will not suit mourners, because it is of a nature to dissolve their mourning. The theologian is like a man sitting in a teacher's chair; the mourner is like a man spending his days on a dunghill in rags. He is not against theology. He is saying that the two postures cannot be occupied simultaneously, and that a man in the middle of repentance who starts systematizing has left the work. He says something similar in Step Twenty-Seven: it is not safe to swim in one's clothes, and a slave of the passions should not touch theology.
Now the passage that the whole movement exists for. When I consider the actual nature of compunction, John says, I am amazed that a thing called mourning and grief should contain joy and gladness woven within it like honey in a comb. And what are we to learn from this? That such compunction is in a special sense a gift of the Lord. There is no pleasureless pleasure in the soul, for God consoles those who are contrite of heart in a secret way.
He then traces the sequence. Tears over our departure produce fear. When fear begets fearlessness, joy dawns. And when joy is unfailingly obtained, holy love bursts into flower. That is the whole arc of the book in one sentence, and it is placed here, at rung seven, twenty-three rungs before the summit, which tells you that John thought the ascent was visible from the bottom.
And then, having spent an entire chapter on weeping, he says the thing that keeps the Ladder from being a monument to sadness. My friends, God does not ask or desire that a man should mourn out of sorrow of heart, but rather that out of love for him he should rejoice with spiritual laughter. Remove sin, and the tear of sorrow becomes superfluous for the eyes. What is the use of a bandage where there is no wound? Before his transgression, Adam had no tears, and there will be none after the resurrection, when sin is abolished, for then pain and sorrow and sighing will have fled away.
The chapter closes with the sentence John chose to end on, and it is worth registering how strange it is. When our soul leaves this world, he says, we shall not be blamed for having worked no miracles, nor for not having been theologians or contemplatives. But we shall certainly have to give an account to God of why we have not unceasingly mourned. He is entirely serious. On his account of the matter, the one thing a person will be asked about is whether he ever took an honest look at himself.
Movement Three: The Loud Passions
The next ten rungs, from Step Eight to Step Seventeen, are the long middle of the book: the war against the passions that a person can see in himself. John's method here is consistent. He defines the vice precisely, he traces its parentage and its offspring, he distinguishes the beginning from the middle from the end of the corresponding virtue, and twice he does something more theatrical, putting the vice on trial and forcing it to testify against itself.
Steps Eight and Nine: Anger and Resentment
Freedom from anger, John says, is an insatiable appetite for dishonour, exactly as in the vainglorious there is an unbounded desire for praise. It is victory over nature and insensibility to insults, acquired by struggle and sweat. Meekness is an immovable state of soul that stays the same in evil report and good report, in dishonour and in praise. And then the three stages: the beginning of freedom from anger is silence of the lips when the heart is agitated; the middle is silence of the thoughts when there is a mere disturbance of soul; and the end is an imperturbable calm under the breath of unclean winds. Those are three genuinely different achievements, and the ordering is exact. Shutting your mouth is the entry-level skill.
He notes the speed problem. There is a quick movement of a millstone, he says, that in one moment grinds up more spiritual grain than another crushes in a whole day. A sudden blaze fanned by a strong wind ruins more of the field of the heart than a lingering flame. This is his answer to the person who says that his temper is short-lived and therefore harmless.
He is honest about one accidental benefit of anger, and he is nearly alone among ascetic writers in admitting it. Though we know very many intolerable fruits of anger, he says, we have found one involuntary offspring which, though illegitimate, is nevertheless useful. I have seen people flaring up madly and vomiting out their long-stored malice who were, by that very passion, delivered from passion, and who obtained from the person who had offended them either an apology or an explanation of the long-standing grievance. And I have seen others who showed a kind of brute patience while nursing resentment under cover of silence, and I considered them more pitiable than the ravers, because they were driving away the holy white Dove with black gall.
The community, in John's view, is the treatment. As a hard stone with sharp corners has all its sharpness knocked off by rubbing against other stones and is made round, so a sharp and curt soul, living in community and mixing with hard, hot-tempered men, undergoes one of two things. Either it cures its wound by patience, or by withdrawing it discovers its own weakness, its cowardly flight showing this to it as in a mirror. He is quite specific that the angry person should not be allowed into solitude. Sitting outside a monastery once, near the cells of some who lived alone, he heard them fighting by themselves inside their cells like caged partridges, leaping at the face of an offender who was not there, and he advised them, devoutly, not to stay in solitude, in case they turned from human beings into demons.
He gives a rule for correction that is easy to remember and hard to keep. If you want to take a splinter out of another person, do not hack at it with a stick instead of a lancet, because you will only drive it in deeper. And the stick is rude speech and rough gestures; the lancet is tempered instruction and patient reprimand. The Apostle said reprove, rebuke, exhort. He did not say beat.
Then the courtroom scene. John binds anger with the chains of meekness, has it beaten by patience and dragged out by holy love, arraigns it before the court of reason, and interrogates it. Tell us, base idiot, what is the name of the father who begot you, and the mother who brought you into the world, and the names of your foul sons and daughters. And also the names of those who wage war on you and kill you. And anger replies: many are my origins, and I have more than one father. My mothers are vainglory, love of money, greed, and sometimes lust. My father is called conceit. My daughters are remembrance of wrongs, hatred, enmity, and assertion of rights. But my opponents, who are now holding me captive, are the opposite virtues of freedom from anger and meekness. She who schemes against me is called humility. As for who bore humility, ask her in due time yourself.
That answer is the map for the next several rungs, and note the last daughter in the list: assertion of rights. John puts insisting on what one is owed in the same family as hatred and enmity, and he is not making a mistake.
Step Nine follows anger's eldest daughter, the remembrance of wrongs. His definition piles up: it is the consummation of anger, the keeper of sins, hatred of righteousness, ruin of virtues, poison of the soul, worm of the mind, shame of prayer, stopping of supplication, estrangement of love, a nail stuck in the soul, a pleasureless feeling cherished in the sweetness of bitterness, continuous sin, unsleeping transgression, hourly malice.
He gives it a short chapter on purpose, saying it is one of the passions that is produced but produces nothing, and that killing the father kills the child: he who has put a stop to anger has also destroyed the remembrance of wrongs, because childbirth continues only while the father is alive.
The test he provides is the sharpest diagnostic tool in the book, and it applies to anybody, monk or not. You will know that you have completely got rid of this rot, he says, not when you pray for the person who offended you, nor when you exchange presents with him, nor when you invite him to your table, but only when, on hearing that he has fallen into some misfortune of body or soul, you suffer and weep for him as for yourself. Every earlier stage on that list is a real achievement and none of them is the finish line. He adds two more lines worth keeping: the remembrance of wrongs is an interpreter of Scripture of the kind that adjusts the words of the Spirit to its own views. And: the forgetting of wrongs is a sign of true repentance, but the man who dwells on them and thinks he is repenting is like a man who dreams that he is running.
Steps Ten to Twelve: Slander, Talkativeness, Lying
Slander, John says, is an offspring of hatred, a subtle yet coarse disease, a leech that lurks unfelt, wasting and draining the blood of charity, a simulation of love, and the ruin of chastity. He records having rebuked people for slandering and being told, in self-defence, that they were doing it out of love and care for the person they were slandering.
His remedy is a single sentence you can actually say out loud. Do not pay attention to the feelings of a person who speaks to you disparagingly about his neighbour, but say to him: stop, brother. I fall into graver sins every day, so how can I criticize him? In this way, John says, you achieve two things: you heal yourself and your neighbour with one plaster. And this is one of the shortest roads to the forgiveness of sins, which is simply not to judge.
He argues the point from an epistemological angle as well as a moral one. Some have fallen openly into great sins, he says, but have done greater good deeds in secret, so that their critics were tricked, getting smoke instead of the sun. He tells of a man he had known who sinned openly and repented secretly, whom John condemned as a profligate, and who was chaste before God, having appeased him by a genuine conversion. And the flat instruction: do not condemn, even if you see it with your own eyes, because eyes are often deceived. He also names the mechanism by which the habit propagates. The demons push us into sin, and if they fail at that, they get us to pass judgment on those who are sinning, so that they may smear us with the very stain we are condemning in someone else.
Step Eleven asks where slander comes out of, and answers: the mouth. Talkativeness, John says, is the throne of vainglory on which it loves to display itself. It is a sign of ignorance, a door to slander, a guide to jesting, a servant of falsehood, the ruin of compunction, a creator of despondency, a precursor of sleep, the dissipation of recollection, the abolition of watchfulness, the cooling of ardour, and the darkening of prayer.
Against it he sets deliberate silence, and his definition of silence is one of the most beautiful pieces of prose in the book: the mother of prayer, a recall from captivity, the preservation of fire, a supervisor of thoughts, a watch against enemies, a prison of mourning, a friend of tears, effective remembrance of death, a depicter of punishment, an aid to anguish, an enemy of freedom of speech, a companion of quiet, an opponent of the desire to teach, an increase of knowledge, a creator of contemplation, unseen progress, secret ascent.
He also gives the causes of chatter, and one of them is mechanical: the tongue, being a member of the body like the rest, requires the training of habit. In ascetics, garrulity comes especially from vainglory and sometimes from gluttony. Which is why, he says, many who forcibly bridle the stomach afterwards find the tongue easy to check. Two members, one restraint.
Step Twelve is on lying, and it is short and unusually blunt. The offspring of flint and steel is fire, John says, and the offspring of chatter and joking is lying. A lie is the destruction of love, and perjury is a denial of God. He grades the offence: there is one judgment for a man who lies out of fear of punishment and another for a man who lies with no danger at hand. One lies for wantonness, another for amusement, one to make bystanders laugh, another to trap his brother.
The passage worth carrying is about the funny man. John says he has seen people who prided themselves on their skill in lying and who, exciting laughter by their jokes and twaddle, pitiably destroyed the habit of mourning in their hearers. And when the demons see that we intend from the start to keep clear of a coarse leader's witty lecture as though it were an infectious disease, they try to catch us with two thoughts: do not offend the story-teller, and, do not appear to love God more than they do. Be off, John says. Do not linger, or the jokes will come back to you at the time of your prayer. He gives one clean marker of a soul in good order: a babe knows nothing of lying, and neither does a soul stripped of evil. And one more: a man who has become merry with wine involuntarily tells the truth about everything, and a man who is drunk with compunction cannot lie.
Step Thirteen: Despondency, the Noonday Demon
The Greek word is akedia, and it is one of the eight capital vices. The translation renders it despondency, and the footnote gives the range: languor, torpor, tedium, spiritual gloom, low spirits, indifference to the work of salvation, distaste for spiritual things, spiritual sloth. It is not sadness and it is not depression in the modern clinical sense. It is the specific collapse of motivation that attacks a person in the middle of a long commitment.
John's definition: a slackness of soul, a weakening of the mind, neglect of asceticism, hatred of the vow one has made. And then a phrase that identifies it instantly to anyone who has felt it: it accuses God of being merciless and without love for men. It is languid in singing psalms, weak in prayer, stubbornly bent on service, resolute in manual labour, and indifferent in obedience. Look at that list carefully. Despondency does not make a man lazy in everything. It makes him energetic about anything except the thing he committed to.
John's account of its timing is famous, and it is the source of the phrase noonday demon. A doctor visits the sick in the morning, he says, but despondency visits ascetics about noon. At the third hour the demon of despondency produces shivering, headache, and even colic. At the ninth hour the sick man gathers his strength. And when the table is laid, he jumps out of bed. But the hour of prayer comes, and again the body is weighed down. He had begun to pray, and it steeps him in sleep, and tears his responses to shreds with untimely yawns.
What makes his portrait genuinely acute is the observation that despondency disguises itself as virtue. It is a pretext for hospitality, John says. It insists that by means of manual labour, alms could be given. It urges us eagerly to go and visit the sick, recalling him who said, I was sick and you visited me. It puts it into our heads to go out visiting the dejected and the faint-hearted, and sets one faint-heart to comfort another. It reminds those standing at prayer of necessary duties, and, brutish as it is, it leaves no stone unturned to find some plausible pretext for dragging us away from prayer as if with a halter. That is the modern experience of it precisely: not the temptation to do nothing, but the sudden and urgent appearance of a more useful task.
He rates it as the worst of the eight for a monk. Each of the other passions is destroyed by some particular virtue, he says, but despondency for a monk is a general death. And correspondingly: nothing procures so many crowns for a monk as the battle with despondency. Spiritual heroes come to light at the time of despondency.
He notices the escalation. Observe, and you will find that if you stand on your feet, despondency will fight with you. If you sit, it will suggest that you would be better off leaning back, and it urges you to lean against the wall of the cell. Then it persuades you to look out of the window, by producing noises and footsteps outside. And he names a timing tell: when there is no psalmody, despondency does not appear, and as soon as the appointed Office is finished, the eyes open.
The remedies he gives are all physical and mental rather than emotional: bind this tyrant with the remembrance of your sins, buffet him with manual labour, bring him into court by the thought of the blessings to come. And then, as with anger, he interrogates it. Tell me, you nerveless, shuffling fellow, who spawned you? Who are your offspring? Who are your foes? Who is your destroyer? And despondency answers: among those who are truly obedient I have nowhere to lay my head, but where I have a place for myself, I live quietly. I have many mothers: sometimes insensibility of soul, sometimes forgetfulness of the things above, sometimes excessive troubles. My offspring who live with me are changing from place to place, disobedience to one's spiritual father, forgetfulness of the judgment, and sometimes breach of the vow. My opponents, who now hold me bound, are psalmody and manual labour. My enemy is the thought of death. What completely mortifies me is prayer with firm hope of future blessings.
Two of those items deserve underlining. Changing from place to place is despondency's firstborn, which means that the impulse to relocate is a symptom, not a solution. And psalmody and manual labour are the named antidotes, which is to say: singing and working with your hands, not resolve.
Step Fourteen: The Stomach
The title John gives this rung is On the clamorous yet wicked master, the stomach, and he opens by admitting that he does not expect to win. We have been attacking ourselves in everything we have said, he begins, but this is especially so when we speak about the stomach. For I wonder whether anyone has got free of this master before settling in the grave.
His definitions are witty in a way the rest of the book usually is not. Gluttony is hypocrisy of the stomach, because when it is glutted it complains of scarcity, and when it is loaded and bursting it cries out that it is hungry. Gluttony is a deviser of seasonings, a source of sweet dishes: you stop one jet and it bobs up elsewhere; you plug that too and you open another. Gluttony is a delusion of the eyes, which receives in moderation but wants to gobble everything at once. And he draws the connection that governs his placement of this rung immediately before the rung on chastity: satiety in food is the father of fornication, and mortification of the stomach is an agent of purity.
He is funny about the monk who plans ahead. The Jew rejoices on Sabbaths and feast days, he says, and a monk who is a glutton on Saturdays and Sundays. He counts the days to Easter beforehand and prepares the food several days in advance. The slave of his belly calculates which dishes he will use to celebrate the feast; the servant of God considers which graces he might be enriched by. And he catches the man who uses hospitality as cover: if a stranger comes, the slave of the stomach is moved to love entirely out of gluttony, and regards laxity for himself as consolation for his brother.
The most instructive passage in Step Fourteen is where John openly overrules an authority. Evagrius, an earlier and enormously influential ascetic writer, had written: when your soul desires different foods, confine it to bread and water. John rejects this outright. To prescribe that, he says, is like telling a child to go up the whole ladder in one stride. So, rejecting his rule, let us say instead: when our soul desires different foods, it is demanding what is proper to its nature.
And then he gives a graduated method in its place. Use cunning against an unscrupulous foe. Unless a very severe conflict is on us, or we are making amends for a fall, let us for a while deny ourselves only fattening foods, then heating foods, and only then whatever makes our food pleasant. That is three stages, in order, each one narrower than the last. And, remarkably: if possible, give your stomach satisfying and digestible food, so as to satisfy its insatiable hunger by sufficiency, and so that quick assimilation may deliver us from excessive desire as from a scourge. His reasoning is that most foods which inflate the stomach also excite the body, so the point is not minimum quantity but the right kind, taken to actual sufficiency. Anyone who has watched a rigid diet collapse into a binge will recognize what John is doing here. He is designing for adherence.
The mechanics he describes are physical. Leather bottles get greater capacity if they are kept supple, and lose it if they are neglected. A man who burdens his stomach with food distends his inside; a man who wars with his stomach contracts it. And when the inside is contracted, we cannot take much, and in future we become fasters naturally. He notes too that thirst is often stopped by thirst, but it is difficult, and perhaps impossible, to cut off hunger by hunger. So when the stomach overcomes you, tame it by labour, and if you are too weak for labour, struggle with it by keeping vigil. If your eyes become heavy, take up handwork; but if sleep is not on you, do not touch handwork, because it is impossible to occupy the mind with God and with manual labour at the same time.
He also gives the timing of the mind's own settling, which is a genuinely useful observation. Keep watch over yourself early in the morning, at midday, and for an hour before taking food, and you will realize the value of fasting. In the morning, thought leaps and runs from one thing to another. Toward the sixth hour it becomes quieter. And by sunset it is completely at peace.
His definition of fasting is a long and generous list, and it is the most positive thing in the chapter. Fasting, he says, is the coercion of nature and the cutting out of everything that delights the palate, the prevention of lust, the uprooting of bad thoughts, deliverance from dreams, purity of prayer, the light of the soul, the guarding of the mind, deliverance from blindness, the door of compunction, humble sighing, glad contrition, a lull in chatter, a means to silence, a guard of obedience, the lightening of sleep, health of body, an agent of dispassion, the remission of sins, and the gate of Paradise and its delight.
Then the second interrogation. John summons gluttony and calls it the door of passions, the fall of Adam, the ruin of Esau, the destruction of the Israelites, the laying bare of Noah's shame, the reproach of Lot, the death of the sons of Eli. And gluttony, foaming with fury, answers: why are you, who are my underlings, overwhelming me with reproaches? How are you trying to get separated from me? I am bound to you by nature. The door for me is the nature of foods. The cause of my insatiability is habit. The foundation of my passion is repeated habit, insensibility of soul, and forgetfulness of death. My first-born son is a minister of fornication, the second is hardness of heart, and the third is sleepiness. My daughters are laziness, talkativeness, familiarity in speech, jesting, facetiousness, contradiction, a stiff neck, obstinacy, disobedience, insensibility, captivity, conceit, audacity, and boasting, after which follow impure prayer, whirling of thoughts, and often sudden misfortunes, with which despair, the most evil of all my daughters, is closely bound. The remembrance of falls resists me but does not conquer me. The thought of death is always hostile to me. But there is nothing among men that destroys me completely.
The confession ends by naming the only thing that beats it. He who has received the Comforter prays to him against me, and the Comforter, when appealed to, does not allow me to act passionately. But those who have not tasted his gift inevitably seek their pleasure in my sweetness. Even here, at the most physical rung on the ladder, John will not let the outcome rest on willpower.
Step Fifteen: Chastity
This is the longest chapter after Step Four, and it is the one where a modern reader will be most tempted to look away. John treats the body as an adversary bound to him by nature, uses language about the flesh that is unsparing, and is candid about sexual sin, including sin against nature, in ways that sit strangely on the page. It is presented here as he wrote it.
His definitions place purity above rules. Purity, he says, means putting on the angelic nature. It is the longed-for house of Christ and the earthly heaven of the heart. It is a supernatural denial of nature, in which a mortal and corruptible body rivals the celestial spirits in a truly marvellous way. He is pure who expels love with love and has extinguished the material fire by the immaterial fire. And chastity, he says, is the name common to all the virtues.
And then the crucial theological move, which prevents the whole chapter from becoming a manual of self-mastery. Let no one thoroughly trained in purity attribute its attainment to himself, John says, for it is impossible for anyone to conquer his own nature. When nature is defeated, it should be recognized that this is due to the presence of him who is above nature, because beyond all dispute the weaker gives way to the stronger. And later, twice more: a man who has resolved to contend with his flesh and conquer it by himself struggles in vain. Offer to the Lord the weakness of your nature, fully acknowledging your own incapacity, and you will imperceptibly receive the gift of chastity. He does not think this rung is climbed. He thinks it is given to people who have stopped pretending they can climb it.
He is also alert to the ways abstinence goes wrong. Do not trust to abstinence not to fall, he says: one who had never eaten was cast out of heaven, meaning the devil. He observes that with beginners falls usually happen through luxury, with intermediates through haughtiness as well, and with those approaching perfection, solely through judging their neighbour. He is unimpressed by mere physical incapacity: I have seen people who fell unwillingly, and I have seen people who would willingly fall and cannot, and I pitied the second group much more, because even though impotent, they yearn for the stench. And he warns against arguing with the temptation directly: do not expect to confute this demon by argument, because with nature on his side, he has the best of the argument.
The most analytically valuable passage in this step is the anatomy of consent that he takes from earlier Fathers, because it is a piece of moral psychology far more careful than anything the reader will be expecting. They distinguish, John reports, between attraction, intercourse, consent, captivity, struggle, and passion in the strict sense. Attraction is a simple conception, or an image of something encountered for the first time, which has lodged in the heart. Intercourse is conversation with what has presented itself, accompanied either by passion or by dispassion. Consent is the bending of the soul to what has been presented, accompanied by delight. Captivity is a forcible and involuntary abduction of the heart, or a lasting association with the thing encountered which destroys the good order of our condition. Struggle is a power equal to the attacking force, which either wins or loses according to the soul's desire. And passion, in the strict sense, is what lurks disquietingly in the soul for a long time and, through its intimacy with the soul, brings it finally to what amounts to a habit, a self-incurred desertion. Of these, John notes, the first is without sin, the second not always, and the third is sinful or sinless according to the condition of the contestant. Struggle is the occasion of crowns or of punishments, and captivity is judged differently depending on the case.
The reason that list matters is that it locates guilt precisely rather than diffusely. A thought arriving is not a sin. Entertaining it may or may not be. Delighting in it is the point at which something has been decided. That is a far more usable framework than a general sense of contamination, and it is the standard by which the Christian East has assessed thoughts ever since.
John then adds something subtler still, which he says the most precise and discerning Fathers noticed. Some of them speak of what they call a flick of the mind. Its characteristic is this: without any passage of time, without word or image, it instantaneously introduces the passion to its victim. There is nothing swifter than this in the material world and nothing less discernible in the spiritual. It shows itself in the soul as a simple remembrance which the soul has no time to dally with, because it is independent of time, unconnected with any image, and impervious to analysis, and in some cases even unknown to the person himself. If anyone, with the help of mourning, has managed to detect such a subtlety, John says, let him explain to us how it is possible for a soul, by a mere glance, or the touch of a hand, or the hearing of a song, with no notion or thought at all, to commit a definite sin of impurity. He is describing something faster than deliberation. He does not claim to have resolved it.
He reports the standing debate about direction of causation, and refuses to settle it. Some say the passions invade the body from thoughts of fornication; others say the opposite, that evil thoughts are born from the feeling of the body. The first group argue that if the mind had not gone first, the body would not have followed. The second point to bodily passion arising from a pleasant sight, the touch of a hand, the smell of perfume, or the hearing of sweet voices. If anyone can settle this in the Lord, John says, let him explain it, because this sort of knowledge is extremely necessary for those living the active life scientifically. But for those practising virtue in simplicity of heart, it is not of the least importance. Not everyone has knowledge, but neither does everyone have the blessed simplicity that is a breastplate against the wiles of evil spirits.
He gives practical counsel that is mostly about circumstance rather than resolve. Let the remembrance of death and the Prayer of Jesus, said as a single repeated phrase, go to sleep with you and rise with you, because you will find nothing to equal these aids during sleep. Touch alone is sufficient for defilement, because nothing is as dangerous as that sense. Avoid by every means either seeing or hearing of that fruit which you have vowed not to taste, because it is absurd to think ourselves stronger than the prophet David. And do not get into the habit of thinking over during the day the phantasies that came to you in sleep, because the aim of the demons in prompting that is to defile you while you are awake by making you think about your dreams.
One elder gave him an explanation of nocturnal emissions that is notable for how many non-physical causes it includes. They come from an abundance of food and from a life of ease, the man said; they also come from contempt, when we pride ourselves on not having been subject to them for a long time; and they come from judging our neighbour. John adds that if a man cannot find any of those three causes in himself, he is blessed to be so free, and if it happens to him anyway it comes solely from the envy of the demons, and God permits it for a time so that after a sinless mishap he may obtain the most sublime humility.
He also identifies pride hiding underneath apparent purity, and this is the most penetrating paragraph in the step. When we have struggled hard with this demon and driven it out of the heart, he says, then this wretch, like a kind of worm, hides in the body and tries to defile us there. And those who are subject to the demon of arrogance especially suffer this way, because as their hearts are no longer continually occupied with impure thoughts, they become prone to pride. Let them examine themselves discreetly once they have achieved a measure of quiet, John says, and they will certainly find a thought concealed in the depth of the heart like a snake in dung, suggesting that they have made progress in purity by their own effort and zeal. Poor wretches. They do not think of what was said: what have you that you did not receive as a free gift?
One more passage, on the sheer instability of the standard. Someone told John of an extraordinarily high degree of purity. A certain man, on seeing a beautiful body, immediately glorified the Creator, and from that one look he was moved to the love of God and to a fountain of tears. And it was wonderful to see, John says, how what would have been a cause of destruction for one man was for another the supernatural cause of a crown. And if such a person always behaves the same way on similar occasions, he has risen immortal before the general resurrection. John applies the same rule to music: lovers of God are moved to holy gaiety, to divine love, and to tears by both worldly and spiritual songs, but lovers of pleasure are moved to the opposite by the same songs. The stimulus is not the variable. The person is.
The chapter ends with a dialogue between the soul and the flesh that is, formally, the most striking thing in the whole book. John asks a series of questions that he does not pretend to answer. How can I hate someone whom by nature I habitually love? How can I get free of one to whom I am bound forever? How can I escape what will share my resurrection? How am I to make immortal what has received a mortal nature? What argument can I use against one who has the argument of nature on his side? If I bind him by fasting, then by condemning my neighbour I am handed over to him again. If I stop judging and so overmaster him, then being proud of that, I am subjected to him again. For he is an ally and a foe, an assistant and a rival, a defender and a traitor. If I humour him he attacks me. If I exhaust him he gets feeble. When he is rested, he misbehaves. If I turn away in loathing, he cannot bear it. If I mortify him, I endanger myself. If I strike him down, I have nothing left with which to obtain the virtues. I embrace him and I turn away from him. What is this mystery in me? What is the meaning of this blending of body and soul? How am I constituted a friend and a foe to myself?
And then he lets the flesh answer, and its answer is not what a reader expects, because it is entirely cooperative. I shall never tell you anything you do not know equally well, it says, but only things we both know. I have my father within me, self-love. The fire I feel from outside comes from being humoured and from general comfort. The fire that burns within comes from past ease and bygone deeds. Having conceived, I give birth to sins, and they, once born, beget death or despair. If you know the deep and obvious weakness that is in both you and me, you have bound my hands. If you starve your appetite, you have bound my feet from going further. If you take the yoke of obedience, you have thrown off my yoke. If you obtain humility, you have cut off my head.
Four handles, in ascending order of effect: honest acknowledgement of weakness ties the hands, restraint of appetite ties the feet, obedience removes the yoke, and humility takes the head off. The body itself gives away that the decisive weapon is not asceticism. It is humility. Which is exactly where the ladder is going.
Steps Sixteen and Seventeen: Money, and Freedom From It
John pairs these two rungs, giving the disease and then the remedy, and he is brief about both.
Avarice, or love of money, he says, is the worship of idols, a daughter of unbelief, an excuse for infirmities, a foreboder of old age, a harbinger of drought, a herald of hunger. The lover of money sneers at the Gospel and is a wilful transgressor. He who has attained love scatters his money, and he who says he lives for both love and money has deceived himself.
The sentence in this step that does the most work is about the trajectory of a motive. The beginning of the love of money is the pretext of almsgiving, he says, and the end of it is hatred of the poor. So long as a man is collecting, he is charitable; when the money is in hand, he tightens his hold. And do not say that you are collecting money for the poor, John adds, because the Kingdom was purchased with two mites. He also notices the strange effect of proximity: I have seen how men of scanty means enriched themselves by living with the poor in spirit, and forgot their first poverty.
Step Seventeen turns it over. Poverty, John says, is the resignation of cares, life without anxiety, an unencumbered traveller, alienation from sorrow, fidelity to the commandments. A poor monk is lord of the world: he has entrusted his cares to God, and by faith he has obtained all men as his servants. He will not tell his need to any man, and he receives what comes to him as from the hand of the Lord. A poor man is pure during prayer, but an acquisitive man prays to material images.
He draws a distinction here between two kinds of renunciation, and prefers the harder one. Great is he who piously renounces possessions, John says, but holy is he who renounces his own will. The one will receive a hundredfold, either in money or in graces; the other will inherit eternal life. And he adds a warning against pointless austerity: a man who impoverishes himself for no reason suffers a double harm, because he abstains from present goods and is deprived of future ones.
Two closing observations from this pair. First, the practical test: he who despises what is material is rid of quarrels and controversies, but the covetous man will fight to the death over a needle. Second, the case he cites: in Job there was no trace of avarice, and therefore when he lost everything, he remained undisturbed. John names the underlying virtue detachment, and says it is born of experience, of a taste of God, and of meditation on the account to be given at death. Some, he says, have burned a great deal of wood with a small fire, and with the help of that one virtue some have escaped all the passions.
Movement Four: The Quiet Passions
Steps Eighteen through Twenty-Three deal with what John considers the more dangerous half of the war, because these vices do not look like vices. Two of them, vainglory and pride, he treats as the same disease at two ages, and he regards them as the reason that spiritually advanced people fail.
Step Eighteen: Insensibility
The full title John gives this rung is worth hearing: On insensibility, that is, the deadening of the soul and the death of the mind before the death of the body. He defines it as deadened feeling, which from long sickness and negligence lapses into loss of feeling. Or: negligence that has become habit, benumbed thought, the birth of presumption, a snare for zeal, the noose of courage, ignorance of compunction, a door to despair, and the mother of forgetfulness, which gives birth to loss of the fear of God, so that insensibility then becomes the daughter of her own daughter.
Then he gives a portrait, and it is the funniest and most uncomfortable passage in the book, because it is unmistakably a portrait of the religious reader. The man who has lost sensibility, John says, is a brainless philosopher, a self-condemned commentator, a self-contradictory windbag, a blind man teaching others to see. He talks about healing a wound and does not stop irritating it. He complains of sickness and does not stop eating what harms him. He prays against a thing and immediately goes and does it, and when he has done it, he is angry with himself, and is not ashamed of his own words. I am doing wrong, he cries, and eagerly continues to do so. His mouth prays against his passion and his body struggles for it. He philosophizes about death and behaves as if he were immortal. He groans over the separation of soul from body and drowses along as if he were eternal. He talks of temperance and self-control and lives for gluttony. He reads about the judgment and begins to smile. He reads about vainglory and is vainglorious while actually reading. He repeats what he has learned about keeping vigil and drops asleep on the spot. He praises prayer and runs from it as from the plague. He blesses obedience and is the first to disobey. He praises detachment and is not ashamed to be spiteful and to fight over a rag. When angered he gets bitter, and then he is angered again at his own bitterness, and does not feel that after one defeat he is suffering another. Having overeaten, he repents, and a little later gives way again. He blesses silence and praises it with a spate of words. He teaches meekness and gets angry during the actual teaching. He condemns laughter and lectures on mourning with a smile on his face. In front of other people he blames himself for being vainglorious, and in blaming himself he is only angling for glory. All the time, John says, he is his own accuser, and he does not want to come to his senses. I will not say cannot.
Then he does something unusual: he admits that he has this one. As far as my poor powers and knowledge allow, he says, I have exposed the wiles of this stony, obstinate, raging, and stupid passion. I have not the patience to expatiate on it. He who is experienced and able in the Lord should not shrink from applying healing to the sores, for I am not ashamed to admit my own powerlessness, since I am sorely afflicted with this sickness. I should not have been able to discover its tricks by myself if I had not caught it and held it firmly, probing it to make it acknowledge what has been said above, and plying it with the scourge of the fear of the Lord and with unceasing prayer.
And so it too is interrogated, and it gives away its own weakness. My subjects laugh when they see corpses, it says. When they stand at prayer they are completely stony, hard, and darkened. When they see the holy altar they feel nothing. When they receive the Gift, it is as if they had eaten ordinary bread. When I see people moved by compunction, I mock them. I am the mother of laughter, the nurse of sleep, the friend of a full belly. When exposed I do not grieve. I go hand in hand with sham piety.
John asked it who its father was, and the answer is the whole diagnosis. I have no single parentage, it said. My conception is mixed and indefinite. Satiety nourishes me, time makes me grow, and bad habit entrenches me. He who keeps this habit will never be rid of me. Be constant in vigil, meditating on the eternal judgment, and then perhaps I shall relax my hold on you to some extent. Find out what caused me to be born in you, and then battle against my mother, for she is not the same in every case. Pray often at the coffins, and engrave an indelible image of them in your heart, for unless you inscribe it there with the pencil of fasting, you will never conquer me.
Two things in that confession are worth extracting. First, the instruction to find out what caused me to be born in you, because she is not the same in every case: John's insensibility has no single cure, and the treatment depends on the individual origin. Second, the honest admission that vigil will relax its hold only to some extent. This is the one rung on which John reports partial results.
Steps Nineteen and Twenty: Sleep and Vigil
These two short rungs are the practical hinge of the daily rule, and the interesting content is the reconnaissance. John catalogues, with evident amusement, the specific tactics used against a man trying to get up and pray. Some stand by our bed and when we get up urge us to lie down again: wait, they say, until the preliminary hymns are finished, then you can go to church. Others plunge those already standing at prayer into sleep. Some produce severe, unusual pains in the stomach. Others get us to make conversation in church. Some entice the mind to shameful thoughts. Others make us lean against the wall as though from fatigue. Sometimes they involve us in fits of yawning. Some bring on waves of laughter during prayer, in order to stir up God's anger against us. Some force us to hurry the reading merely out of laziness; others suggest we sing more slowly for the pleasure of it; and sometimes they sit at our mouths and shut them, so that we can scarcely open them.
His counter to all of it is a single realization rather than a technique. He who realizes that he is standing before God will be as still as a pillar during prayer and will pray with heartfelt feeling, and none of the aforesaid demons will make sport of him.
He also grades the ways of keeping a night vigil, and it is characteristic that he does not insist on one. Some lift up their hands in prayer as if they were bodiless and stripped of all care. Others stand singing psalms. Others are more occupied in reading. Some, out of weakness, courageously resist sleep by working with their hands. Others try to feel the horror of the thought of death, hoping to obtain contrition that way. Of all these, John says, the first and the last are keeping vigil for the love of God, the second do what befits a monk, and the third go the lowest way. And then: yet God accepts and values the offerings of each according to their intention and their power.
His definition of vigil is compact and it names its real function. Vigil, he says, is a quenching of lust, deliverance from dream phantoms, a tearful eye, a softened heart, the guarding of thoughts, the dissolving of food, the subduing of the passions, the taming of spirits, the bridling of the tongue, and the banishment of phantasies. A monk who denies himself sleep is a fisher of thoughts, and in the stillness of the night he can easily observe and catch them. That is what vigil is for, on John's account: not endurance for its own sake but visibility. The thoughts are catchable at night because there is nothing else in the room.
And two small barbs that give the flavour. The God-loving monk, when the bell rings for prayer, says, good, good. The lazy one says, what a nuisance. And: the inexperienced monk is wide awake in friendly conversation, but his eyes become heavy when the hour of prayer is on him. At the sound of the trumpet the dead will rise, and when idle talk is afoot, those who were asleep come to themselves.
Step Twenty-One: Cowardice
A brief rung, and it is included because John has diagnosed fear as a symptom of something else. Cowardice, he says, is a childish disposition in an old, vainglorious soul. It is a falling away from faith that comes of expecting the unexpected. Fear is a rehearsing of danger beforehand, a trembling sensation of the heart alarmed by unknown misfortunes, a loss of conviction.
His claim is that fear is parasitic on pride. A proud soul is a slave of cowardice: it vainly trusts in itself and is afraid of any sound or shadow. And he offers an observation to prove the point: those who mourn over their sins but are insensible to every other sorrow do not feel cowardice, whereas the cowardly often have breakdowns. He also notes the asymmetry carefully rather than overclaiming: although all cowardly people are vainglorious, not everyone who is unafraid is humble, since robbers and grave-plunderers may also be without fear.
The treatment is exposure, plainly stated. Do not hesitate to go late at night to the places where you usually feel afraid, he says, because if you yield even a little to this weakness, this childish and ridiculous infirmity will grow old with you. As you go, arm yourself with prayer. When you reach the place, stretch out your hands. And when you get rid of the fear, praise him who delivered you, because if you continue to be thankful, he will protect you forever. He is realistic about the timeline: just as it is impossible to satisfy the stomach in one bout, it is impossible to overcome fear instantly.
And the line that reframes the whole rung: it is not darkness and loneliness of place that give the demons power against us, but barrenness of soul.
Step Twenty-Two: Vainglory
John notes at the start that some writers count vainglory and pride as two separate capital sins, making eight, while Gregory the Theologian and others count seven, and he inclines to seven. His reason is a question: who that has conquered vainglory has pride within him? The only difference between them, he says, is the difference between a child and a man, or between wheat and bread. One is the beginning and the other is the end.
His definitions are precise about the loss involved. With regard to its form, vainglory is a change of nature, a perversion of character, a note of blame. With regard to its quality, it is a dissipation of labours, a waste of sweat, a betrayal of treasure, a child of unbelief, the precursor of pride, shipwreck in harbour, and an ant on the threshing floor which, though small, has designs on all one's labour and fruit. The ant waits for the wheat to be gathered, and vainglory waits for the riches of virtue to be gathered, because the one loves to steal and the other to squander.
He then names the structural difference between this vice and all the ones before it. The spirit of despair rejoices at the sight of increasing vice, he says, and the spirit of vainglory at the sight of increasing virtue. The door of the first is a multitude of wounds; the door of the second is a wealth of labours. Which means that everything gained on the previous fourteen rungs is precisely what vainglory has to work with. This is why it comes so late on the ladder.
The most quoted passage in the chapter is his description of being caught either way, and it is worth hearing in his own construction. The sun shines on everyone alike, he says, and vainglory beams on all activities. I am vainglorious when I fast, and when I relax the fast so as not to be noticed, I am vainglorious again over my prudence. When I am well dressed I am overcome by vainglory, and when I put on poor clothes I am vainglorious again. When I talk I am defeated, and when I am silent I am defeated again. However I throw this prickly pear, a spike stands upright.
That is not a counsel of despair; it is a description of why the vice cannot be handled by adjusting outward behaviour, since every adjustment is itself available for display. And John's diagnostic follows from it. God often hides from our eyes even the perfections we have obtained, he says. But the man who praises us, or rather misleads us, opens our eyes by his praise, and as soon as our eyes are opened, our treasure vanishes. He is describing self-consciousness as the loss itself, not as the precursor to a loss.
His picture of the monk performing for visitors is comic and merciless. Vainglory makes monks anticipate the arrival of lay guests and go out of the cloister to meet them. It makes them fall at the visitors' feet and, though full of pride, feign humility. It checks manner and voice, and keeps an eye on the visitors' hands in order to receive something. It calls them lords and patrons, graced with godly life. To those sitting at table it suggests abstinence, and it rebukes subordinates mercilessly. It stirs those who are slack in psalmody to make an effort; those who have no voice become good singers, and the sleepy wake up. It flatters the conductor and begs for first place in the choir, and calls him father and master, as long as the guests are still there.
He notes that the abbot at Alexandria had found the counter-move to this. When the abbot noticed that some monks liked to display themselves in front of visitors from the world, he would, in the presence of those visitors, subject those monks to extreme insults and give them the most humiliating jobs, so that they began to beat a hasty retreat, and the arrival of guests became their victory. Then, John says, an extraordinary spectacle presented itself: vanity chasing herself away and escaping from people.
He includes a story from a man who had the gift of seeing. Once, when I was sitting in assembly, the man said, the demon of vainglory and the demon of pride came and sat beside me, one on each side. The one poked me in the ribs with the finger of vainglory and urged me to relate some vision or some labour I had performed in the desert. But as soon as I had shaken him off, saying, let them be turned back and put to shame who plot evil against me, then the demon on my left said in my ear: well done, well done, you have become great by conquering my shameless mother. So, turning to him, I made apt use of the rest of the verse and said: let them be turned back and put to shame who said to me, well done, well done. That is a whole technique in miniature. The counter to the second demon is not a new idea but the second half of the same sentence.
John asked that man how vainglory is the mother of pride, and got the answer: praises exalt and puff a man up, and when the soul is exalted, then pride seizes it, lifts it to heaven, and casts it down to the abyss.
He distinguishes two glories, and gives a test for telling them apart. There is a glory that comes from the Lord, since he says, those who glorify me I will glorify. And there is a glory that dogs us through diabolic intrigue, since it is said, woe to you when all men speak well of you. You may be sure it is the first kind, John says, when you regard it as harmful and avoid it by every possible means, and hide your manner of life wherever you go. But you will know the second kind when you do something, however trifling, hoping to be observed.
One warning he gives here is worth transplanting anywhere. Abominable vainglory suggests that we should pretend to have some virtue we do not possess, spurring us on with the text: let your light so shine before men that they may see your good works. He is naming the specific abuse of Scripture that vainglory prefers, and he does not soften it.
And his method when praise arrives is immediate and mechanical. When our praisers, or rather our seducers, begin to praise us, let us briefly call to mind the multitude of our own sins, and we shall find ourselves unworthy of what is being said. And if that is insufficient, briefly recollect our death. And if that too is ineffective, at least let us fear the shame that follows honour.
Step Twenty-Three: Pride, and Blasphemous Thoughts
Pride, John says, is denial of God, an invention of the devil, the despising of men, the mother of condemnation, the offspring of praise, a sign of sterility, flight from divine assistance, the precursor of madness, the herald of falls, a foothold for satanic possession, source of anger, door of hypocrisy, the support of demons, the guardian of sins, the patron of unsympathy, the rejection of compassion, a bitter inquisitor, an inhuman judge, an opponent of God, a root of blasphemy.
The three stages here are worth attention because the first one is invisible from outside. The beginning of pride is the consummation of vainglory. The middle is the humiliation of our neighbour, the shameless parade of our labours, complacency in the heart, and hatred of exposure. And the end is denial of God's help, the extolling of one's own exertions, and a fiendish character.
John notices that pride can live inside thanksgiving, which is its cleverest hiding place. This passion often finds food in gratitude, he says, for at first it does not shamelessly advise us to deny God. I have seen people who thank God with their mouths while mentally magnifying themselves. And this is confirmed by the Pharisee who said, ironically, God, I thank thee.
The two exchanges he records are short and devastating. A most learned old man spiritually admonished a proud brother, and the brother, in his blindness, said: excuse me, Father, I am not proud. And the wise old man said: what clearer proof of this passion could you have given us, son, than to say, I am not proud?
And a venerable man said to John: suppose there are twelve shameful passions. If we deliberately love one of them, and I mean pride, it will fill the place of the remaining eleven.
He gives an argument about ownership that is the intellectual core of the chapter. It is shameful to be proud of the adornments of others, he says, but it is utter madness to imagine one deserves God's gifts. Be exalted only by such merits as you had before your birth, because what you got after your birth, and birth itself, God gave you. Only those virtues which you have obtained without the cooperation of the mind belong to you, because your mind was given you by God. Only such victories as you have won without the cooperation of the body have been accomplished by your own efforts, because the body is not yours but a work of God. Follow that reasoning to the end and the set of achievements a person can properly boast about is empty.
And he notes the escalating scale of curability, which is why pride sits at rung twenty-three and not earlier: men can cure the lustful, angels the malicious, but only God the proud. He also observes the peculiar blindness of it. I do not know how it is, he says, but the proud for the most part remain ignorant of their real selves, and imagine they are victorious over their passions, and only realize their poverty when they depart from this life.
The third interrogation of the book takes place here, and it is the one that gives away the whole strategy. John says he caught this mad imposter rising in his heart, carrying its mother, vainglory, on its shoulders. He roped them both with the noose of obedience, thrashed them with the whip of humility, and demanded to know how they had got access to him.
And at last, when flogged, they said: we have neither beginning nor birth, for we are the originators of all the passions. Contrition of heart that is born of obedience is our real enemy. We cannot bear to be subject to anyone; that is why we fell from heaven, though we had authority there. In brief, we are the parents of everything that opposes humility, for everything which furthers humility opposes us. Our power extends everywhere short of heaven, so where will you run from our presence? We often accompany patience under dishonour, and obedience, and freedom from anger, and lack of resentment, and service of one's neighbour. Our offspring are the sins of spiritual people: anger, calumny, spite, irritability, shouting, blasphemy, hypocrisy, hatred, envy, disputing, self-will, disobedience.
Then the confession that the whole ladder has been building toward: there is only one thing in which we have no power to meddle, and we shall tell you this, because we cannot bear your blows. If you keep up a sincere condemnation of yourself before the Lord, you can count us as weak as a cobweb.
Note two things. First, the list of company pride keeps: patience under dishonour, obedience, freedom from anger, lack of resentment, service of one's neighbour. Every item on that list is a rung already climbed. Pride is not the enemy of the ascent; it is the passenger. Second, the single thing it cannot touch is not a virtue that can be performed but an inward act of honest self-accusation, which by its nature cannot be displayed.
Blasphemous Thoughts
John appends to this rung a short section on what he calls unspeakable blasphemy, and it is one of the most pastorally valuable passages in the book, because the phenomenon he describes is still common and still terrifies people who experience it.
The phenomenon is this: intrusive blasphemous words appearing in the mind, at the worst possible moments, and specifically during the Liturgy, at the very moment when the Mysteries are being accomplished. John says this difficult thing is hard to put into words, hard to confess, and hard to expose to a spiritual physician, and that because of that it has produced frustration and despair in many, destroying all their hope like a worm in a tree. It has driven many out of their minds. No other thought, he says, is so difficult to tell in confession, which is why it often stays with people to the very end of their lives.
His argument that these thoughts are not the person's own is straightforward and it is the whole relief he is offering. If these shameless and disgraceful words are my own, he asks, how could I worship after receiving the Gift? How can I praise and revile at one and the same time? He states the conclusion flatly: no one in the face of blasphemous thoughts need think that the guilt lies within him, for the Lord is the knower of hearts and he is aware that such words and thoughts do not come from us but from our foes. And: he who is troubled by the spirit of blasphemy and wants to be delivered from it should know for certain that it is not his own soul that is the cause of such thoughts.
The strategy he prescribes is not resistance but contempt, and he gives his reasons. He who wants to grapple with the demon of blasphemy in any other way, he says, is like a man trying to hold lightning in his hands. How will you contend and grapple with one who bursts into the heart suddenly like the wind, utters words faster than a flash, and immediately vanishes? All the other enemies stop, wrestle, linger, and give time to those who wish to struggle with them. Not this one. He has scarcely appeared and he is gone; he has hardly said a word and he is away. So: he who despises this foe is delivered from its torture, but he who contrives some other way of waging war on it will end by submitting to it. He who wishes to conquer these spirits with words is like a man trying to lock up the winds. And he notes one root cause worth removing: we ought to stop judging and condemning our neighbour, and then blasphemous thoughts will not alarm us, because the first is the occasion and root of the second.
The story he closes with is the tenderest moment in the Ladder. One careful monk, troubled by this demon, wore out his flesh for twenty years with fasts and vigils, and felt no benefit whatsoever. So he wrote his temptation on a card, went to a certain holy man, gave him the card, and bowed his face to the ground, not daring to look up. As soon as the elder had read it, he smiled, and raising the brother up, he said: lay your hand on my neck, son. And when the brother had done that, the great man said: on my neck, brother, be this sin, for as many years as it may or may not be active in you. Only, after this, ignore it. And the monk assured John that even before he had left the elder's cell, his infirmity was gone. John notes that the man who had been tempted told him this himself, offering thanksgiving to God.
Twenty years of asceticism did nothing. One sentence from someone willing to carry it ended it. Whatever mechanism a reader wants to assign to that, John has placed it here deliberately, as the last word before the ladder turns toward humility.
Movement Five: The Summit
The last seven rungs change register. Up to now John has mostly been describing things to do. From Step Twenty-Four onward he is increasingly describing things that happen to a person, and he says so, repeatedly, in the form of an apology for even attempting to write about them.
Step Twenty-Four: Meekness, the Light Before the Sun
John opens with an image that establishes the order of the last movement. The morning light precedes the sun, he says, and the precursor of all humility is meekness. Therefore let us hear in what order the Light arranges these virtues, for he says: learn of me, for I am meek and humble in heart. So before looking at the sun, which is humility, we must be illumined by the light, which is meekness, and then we can look at the sun with a clear gaze. It is impossible, absolutely impossible, to gaze on the sun before we have experienced that light.
His definitions of meekness are more active than the English word suggests. Meekness is an unchangeable state of mind which remains the same in honour and in dishonour. Meekness consists in praying calmly and sincerely for a troublesome neighbour. Meekness is a rock overlooking the sea of irritability, which breaks all the waves that dash against it and yet remains completely unmoved. It is the buttress of patience, the door, or rather the mother, of love, and the foundation of discernment. It is a curb for the furious, a check to the irritable, a minister of joy, the imitation of Christ, something proper to angels, shackles for demons, a shield against peevishness.
He then links it to knowledge in a way that is unexpected. A quiet soul makes room for words of wisdom, he says, because the Lord will guide the meek in judgment, or rather, in discretion. The souls of the meek are filled with knowledge, but an angry mind is a denizen of darkness and ignorance. On John's account, temper is not merely a moral failing; it is an epistemic one. It makes a person stupid.
Paired with meekness in this rung is simplicity, and against both he sets malice, which he defines at length as a perversion of honesty, a deceitful way of thinking falsely screened by affability, an abyss of cunning, deceit that has become a habit, conceit turned into nature, a foe to humility, a pretence of penitence, a diminution of mourning, hostility to confession, a smiling at offences, an affected frowning, a false reverence, a diabolical life.
The distinction John draws about simplicity is important, because he ranks the harder kind higher. Excellent, and blessed, is the simplicity that some people have by nature, he says, but not as much as the simplicity that is grafted in with toil and trouble after repenting from sin. The first is sheltered and protected from much affectation and passion, but the second leads to the highest humility and meekness. The first has not much reward. The second has infinite reward. Recovered innocence, in other words, outranks innocence that was never lost.
And the paradoxical instruction he closes on: it is hard for the rich to enter the Kingdom of Heaven, and equally hard for those who are foolishly wise to enter simplicity. Struggle to elude your own prudence, and by doing so you will find salvation and honesty.
Step Twenty-Five: Humility
This is the rung the book has been organized around. John begins by declaring the task impossible. A man who thinks it is possible to use visible words to describe the sensation of the love of the Lord exactly, or holy humility gracefully, or blessed purity truly, or divine enlightenment clearly, and who imagines that by his description he will enlighten people who have never actually experienced these things, is like a man who wants to give an idea of the sweetness of honey, by words and comparisons, to people who have never tasted it. And just as the second man talks in vain, not to say babbles, so the first either shows that he has no experience of what he is talking about, or else has become the mere toy of vainglory.
Having said that, he convenes a committee. He gathers the definitions his fathers and brothers gave him, and lists them. One said humility means constant oblivion of one's own achievements. Another, the acknowledgement of oneself as the last of all and the greatest sinner of all. Another, the mind's recognition of its own weakness and impotence. Another, that in fits of rage it means to forestall one's neighbour and be first to stop the quarrel. Another, recognition of divine grace and divine mercy. Another, the feeling of a contrite soul and the renunciation of one's own will.
And then John says the honest thing: when I had listened to all of this and had considered it attentively and soberly, I found that I had not been able to comprehend the blessed sense of that virtue from what had been said. So, last of all, having gathered what fell from the lips of those learned and blessed fathers as a dog gathers the crumbs that fall from the table, I gave my own definition. Humility is a nameless grace in the soul, its name known only to those who have learned it by experience. It is unspeakable wealth, a name and a gift from God, for it is said: learn not from an angel, not from man, and not from a book, but from me, that is, from me indwelling, from my illumination and action in you, for I am meek and humble in heart, and your souls shall find rest from conflicts and relief from arguments.
That is the strongest claim in the Ladder, and it needs to be heard clearly: John holds that humility is not a technique and not an attitude, but something that is taught directly by God to a person from inside, and that everything on the previous twenty-four rungs is preparation for receiving instruction that cannot be transmitted any other way.
He does, though, give observable signs, and this is the practical heart of the step. As soon as the cluster of holy humility begins to blossom in us, he says, we at once begin, though with effort, to hate all human glory and praise, and to banish irritation and anger from ourselves. And in proportion as this queen of the virtues progresses in the soul, we begin to regard all the good deeds we have accomplished as nothing, or rather as an abomination.
The three properties he names are worth memorizing as a self-test. The first and paramount property is the acceptance of indignity with the greatest pleasure, when the soul receives it with outstretched hands and welcomes it as something that relieves and cauterizes diseases of the soul. The second is the loss of all bad temper, and modesty at its appeasement. The third and highest is a true distrust of one's own good qualities, together with a constant desire to learn.
And a second set, elsewhere in the step, which is more interior. You will know with certainty that you have this holy possession within you, John says, by an abundance of unspeakable light, by an unutterable love for prayer, and, before either of those is attained, by a heart that does not judge the faults of others. And the precursor of all of it is hatred of every kind of vainglory.
He distinguishes humility carefully from two things that resemble it. Contrition is one thing, self-knowledge is another, and humility is another. Contrition is the result of a fall: the man who falls is crushed and stands in prayer without boldness but with praiseworthy persistence, as one who is shattered, steadying himself with the staff of hope and using it to drive off the dog of despair. Self-knowledge is a true idea of one's own spiritual growth and an unbroken remembrance of one's slightest sins. Humility is the spiritual doctrine of Christ, received spiritually in the closet of the soul by those who are counted worthy of it, and it cannot be explained in visible words.
He gives two practical scenes here that are better than any argument. In the first, the demons appeared in visible form to one of the more discerning brothers and praised him. And this very wise man said to them: if you stop praising me through the thoughts of my heart, I shall conclude from your departure that I am great. But if you continue to praise me, then from your very praise I shall guess at my own impurity, because every proud-hearted man is unclean before the Lord. So either go away from me, and I shall become great, or else praise me, and through you I shall obtain more humility. They vanished immediately.
In the second, an ascetic striving for humility had praise whispered into his heart, and he contrived a pious ruse. He got up and wrote on the wall of his cell the names of the highest virtues in order: perfect love, angelic humility, pure prayer, inviolable chastity, and others like them. And so when the thoughts of vainglory began to praise him, he said to them: let us go and be judged. Then, going to the wall, he read the names, and cried out to himself: when you possess all these, then you will know how far you still are from God.
Two of John's images from this step have outlived the rest. The first is the lemon tree. The natural property of the lemon tree is such that it lifts its branches upward when it has no fruit, and the more the branches bend down, the more fruit they bear. Those who have the mind to understand will grasp the meaning. The second is the valley. Know, beloved, that the valleys shall stand deep in corn and spiritual fruit. This valley is a soul low and humble among the mountains, filled with labours and virtues and always remaining lowly and steadfast. David did not say, I have fasted, or, I have kept vigil, or, I have lain on the bare earth, but: I humbled myself, and soon the Lord saved me.
He makes the case that humility is the shortcut, and means it literally. When you see or hear that someone has acquired the most sublime dispassion in a few years, he says, then conclude that he travelled by no other road than this blessed short cut. Many have received salvation without prophecies and lights, without signs and wonders, but without humility no one will enter the marriage chamber, because humility is the guardian of these gifts, and without her they bring frivolous people to ruin.
And he gives an escape clause that is unusual for him, and generous. Let us hasten with all our powers to fight our way to the crest of humility, he says. Failing that, let us at least mount on her shoulders. And if our effort is not sufficient for that, let us at least not fall out of her arms.
One theological note he adds is the reason the book does not end in despair. If the pride of some of the angels made them demons, he says, then no doubt humility can make angels out of demons. So those who have fallen may take courage. And one instruction for what to do with a neighbour's clearer sight of us: for those of us who do not wish to humble ourselves, the Lord has arranged in his providence that no one can see his own faults as well as his neighbour does. So we are bound to give thanks for our healing not to ourselves but to our neighbour, and to God.
Step Twenty-Six: Discernment
The longest step in the second half, and the most miscellaneous: nearly two hundred short paragraphs on how to tell things apart. John graduates the virtue in three degrees. Discernment in beginners is true knowledge of themselves. In intermediate souls it is a spiritual sense that faultlessly distinguishes what is truly good from what is merely natural and from what is opposed to the good. And in the perfect it is the knowledge they possess by divine illumination, which can enlighten with its lamp whatever is dark in others. Or, generally: discernment is the assured understanding of the divine will on all occasions, in every place, and in all matters, and it is found only in those who are pure in heart, body, and mouth.
The single most useful thing in this step is the account of the three pits. In all our actions in which we try to please God, John says, the demons dig three pits for us. In the first, they endeavour to prevent any good at all from being done. In the second, after their first defeat, they try to secure that it should not be done according to the will of God. And when they fail at that too, then, standing quietly before our soul, they praise us for living a thoroughly godly life. The first is to be opposed by zeal and the fear of death, the second by obedience and humiliation, and the third by unceasing self-condemnation. Three attacks, three counters, in order, and the third attack only becomes available once the first two have been defeated.
He gives an alphabet for beginners, twenty-four items, one for each letter of the Greek alphabet: obedience, fasting, sackcloth, ashes, tears, confession, silence, humility, vigil, courage, cold, toil, hard work, humiliation, contrition, forgetfulness of wrongs, brotherly love, meekness, simple and unquestioning faith, freedom from worldly cares, hateless hatred of parents, detachment, innocent simplicity, and voluntary abasement. And a corresponding scheme for the advanced, which is evidence of their progress: absence of vainglory, freedom from anger, good hope, silence, discernment, firm remembrance of the judgment, compassion, hospitality, moderation in reproof, passionless prayer, and disregard of self.
His image for the frog in the bucket is one of the sharpest things in the book, and it names a problem that no amount of good intention will solve on its own. In drawing water from a well, he says, we sometimes bring up a frog with the water without noticing it, and so in acquiring the virtues we often get involved in the vices that are imperceptibly entwined with them. And then the list of which vice rides which virtue: gluttony is entangled with hospitality; lust with love; cunning with discernment; malice with thoughtfulness; duplicity, procrastination, laziness, contradiction, wilfulness and disobedience with meekness; contempt of instruction with silence; conceit with joy; indolence with hope; harsh judgment with love again; despondency and sloth with quietness; acerbity with chastity; familiarity with humility. And behind all of them, as a general salve, or rather a poison, follows vainglory.
He is remarkable on the question of prayers that are not answered, and he gives four reasons rather than pretending there is one. Everyone who asks and does not obtain from God is denied for one of the following reasons: because they ask at the wrong time, or because they ask unworthily and vaingloriously, or because if they received it they would become conceited, or because they would become negligent after obtaining it. And he adds a sentence that should be set beside every complaint of divine slowness: it would have pleased the Lord if all men in a single moment had become free of passion, only his foresight told him that this would not be for their good.
The section on how to learn God's will is the most practically applicable material in the step. Those who wish to learn the will of the Lord, John says, must first mortify their own will. Then, having prayed to God with faith and honest simplicity, and having asked the fathers or even the brothers with humility of heart and no thought of doubt, they should accept the advice they are given as though it came from the mouth of God, even if that advice is contrary to their own view, and even if the people consulted are not very spiritual. For God is not unjust, and will not lead astray souls who with faith and innocence humbly submit to the advice and judgment of their neighbour.
He then catalogues the other methods people have used, without ranking them dogmatically. Some laid aside all attachments, submitted to God their own thought about a particular inclination, whether to perform an action or resist it, offered fervent prayer for a set number of days, and came to knowledge of his will either through a spiritual communication to their mind or through the complete disappearance from their soul of the intention they had cherished. Others concluded that the trouble and distraction attending an undertaking meant it was not from God. Others, on the contrary, recognized that an action was pleasing to God from its unexpected success. And John adds a caution against dithering: to waver in one's judgments and remain in doubt for a long time without assurance is the sign of an unenlightened and ambitious soul.
He also refuses to let anyone use the extremity of the saints as a reason to quit. A man who despairs of himself when he hears of the supernatural virtues of the saints is most unreasonable, he says. On the contrary, those accounts teach you supremely one of two things: either they rouse you to emulation by their holy courage, or they lead you, by way of thrice-holy humility, to deep self-contempt and the realization of your own inherent weakness. Both outcomes are wins. Neither is despair.
And he is careful about the difference between attempting too much and attempting too little. There are courageous souls who with love and humility of heart throw themselves into tasks beyond them, he says, and there are proud hearts who do the same, because our foes often intentionally suggest to us things beyond our powers so that we lose heart and abandon even what is within them. His resolution: God judges our repentance not by our labours but by our humility. And: great is he who leaves undone nothing that is within his power, but greater is he who humbly attempts what is beyond his power.
One conceptual point in this step is load-bearing for the whole book. There are virtues, John says, and there are mothers of virtues, so a wise man strives rather to obtain the latter. The teacher of the mother-virtues is God himself, through his own action, while there are plenty of teachers for the daughter-virtues. This is why the ladder is ordered the way it is: the rungs are not a list of good qualities to collect but a sequence in which a small number of generative virtues are acquired, and the rest follow.
He ends the step with a chain that runs from the smallest beginning to the summit, and it is the most compressed statement of the book's whole thesis. The lessening of evil breeds abstinence from evil. Abstinence from evil is the beginning of repentance. The beginning of repentance is the beginning of salvation, and the beginning of salvation is a good intention, and a good intention is the mother of labours. The beginning of labours is the virtues; the beginning of the virtues is a flowering, and the flowering of virtue is the beginning of activity. The offspring of virtue is perseverance, and the fruit of persevering practice is habit, and the child of habit is character. Good character is the mother of fear, and fear gives birth to the keeping of the commandments. The keeping of the commandments is a sign of love, and the beginning of love is an abundance of humility, and an abundance of humility is the daughter of dispassion, and the acquisition of dispassion is the fullness of love, that is to say the perfect indwelling of God in those who through dispassion are pure in heart. For they shall see God.
Step Twenty-Seven: Stillness
Step Twenty-Seven is about hesychia, the solitary and inwardly quiet life, and John approaches it with visible reluctance, because he thinks talking about it in front of the wrong audience does harm. He says so explicitly at the start: we do not consider it permissible to talk about peace to the courageous warriors of our King who are still struggling in the battle. He will describe it, briefly, mainly in order to explain what it is.
His definitions separate the two levels. Solitude of the body is the knowledge and reduction to order of one's habits and feelings. Solitude of soul is the knowledge of one's own thoughts and an inviolable mind. A friend of solitude is a courageous and unrelenting power of thought which keeps constant vigil at the doors of the heart and kills or repels the thoughts that come. And, memorably: a solitary is one who strives to confine his bodiless being within his bodily house, paradoxical as that is.
The image he apologizes for is the best one. The cat keeps hold of her mouse, he says, and the thought of the solitary holds his spiritual mouse. Do not call this example rubbish; if you do, then you do not yet know what solitude means. He is describing watchfulness as patient, still, and predatory rather than as relaxation, which is the standard modern misunderstanding of the word.
His warnings are stern and specific. A man who is sick in soul from some passion and attempts solitude is like a man who has jumped from a ship into the sea and thinks he will reach the shore on a plank. He who is still troubled by bad temper and conceit, by hypocrisy and remembrance of wrongs, should never dare set foot on the solitary way, in case he gains distraction and nothing else. He who has not yet known God is unfit for solitude and exposes himself to many dangers. Solitude chokes the inexperienced: not having tasted the sweetness of God, they waste their time being taken captive, robbed, made despondent, and subjected to distractions. And a line that should be pinned up wherever people go on retreat: the solitary who has become lazy will tell lies, urging people by hints to end his solitude for him, and having left his cell, he blames the devils. He has not discovered that he is his own devil.
The most useful part of the step is the audit John performs on motives, because he lists the wrong reasons for withdrawing before the right one. Some enter this harbour, or rather this sea, or perhaps this abyss, because they cannot control their tongue, or because of a past bodily habit. Others because they cannot control their temper and cannot master it in crowded society. Others because out of conceit they have judged it better to sail at their own discretion than under direction. Others because among material things they cannot abstain from them. Some with the intention of cultivating zeal by solitude. Others to torment themselves secretly for their faults. And some in order to acquire glory from it. And then the right reason, stated with a wistfulness that is unmistakable: others again, and John adds, if only the Son of Man when he comes may find such on earth, are wedded to holy solitude out of a delightful thirst for the love and sweetness of God. But they do not achieve that union, he says, before they have divorced all despondency, because fellowship with despondency would be like adultery to anyone who is united with God.
He then hands the reader the self-examination directly. As far as my meagre knowledge permits, he says, for I am like an unskilled architect, I have constructed a ladder of ascent. Let each look to see which step he is standing on. Is it self-will, or human glory, or weakness of tongue, or hot temper, or too great an attachment? Is it to atone for faults, or to grow more fervent?
He gives the signs of getting it right, and they are worth hearing as a list: an unruffled mind, sanctified thought, rapture toward the Lord, recollection of eternal torments, the urgency of death, a constant hunger for prayer, unsleeping vigilance, a wasting away of lust, ignorance of attachment, death to the world, loss of gluttony, a sure understanding of divine things, a well of discernment, a truce accompanied by tears, and loss of talkativeness. And the signs of getting it wrong, which are shorter and more alarming: a dearth of spiritual wealth, an increase of anger, a hoard of resentment, a diminution of love, and a growth of vanity. And I will be silent, he says, about all the rest that follow. Anyone who has withdrawn from other people and then noticed themselves becoming angrier, more resentful, and less loving has been given John's diagnosis in advance.
He gives three tasks in order. The preliminary task of solitude is disengagement from all affairs, whether laudable or not, because a man who allows even the laudable ones will certainly fall into the others. The second task is earnest prayer. And the third is inviolable activity of the heart. It is physically impossible for someone who does not know the alphabet to study books, he says, and it is more impossible still for someone who has not attained the first task to pass rightly to the last two.
One elder, George Arsilaites, gave him a timetable of the day's attacks that is oddly practical: I have noticed, he said, that in the morning it is usually the demons of vainglory and concupiscence who assault us; at midday the demons of despondency, repining, and anger; and in the evening, those dung-loving tyrants of the wretched stomach.
John's counsel on reading in this step is the most direct advice in the book about study, and it cuts against the grain of most religious ambition. Reading enlightens the mind considerably and helps it concentrate, he says, for those are the Holy Spirit's words and they attune those who attend to them. Let what you read lead you to action, because you are a doer. Putting these words into practice makes further reading superfluous. Seek to be enlightened by the words of salvation through your labours, and not merely from books. And then the warning: until you receive spiritual power, do not study works of an allegorical nature, because they are dark words and they darken the weak.
And two lines from this step name the whole discipline. Let the remembrance of Jesus be present with each breath, and then you will know the value of solitude. And: solitude is unceasing worship and waiting upon God.
Step Twenty-Eight: Prayer
John's definition of prayer is the fullest definition of anything in the book. Prayer, by reason of its nature, is the converse and union of man with God, and by reason of its action it upholds the world and brings about reconciliation with God. It is the mother and also the daughter of tears, the propitiation for sins, a bridge over temptations, a wall against afflictions, a crushing of conflicts, the work of angels, the food of all spiritual beings, future gladness, boundless activity, the spring of virtues, the source of graces, invisible progress, the food of the soul, the enlightening of the mind, an axe for despair, a demonstration of hope, the annulling of sorrow, the wealth of monks, the treasure of solitaries, the reduction of anger, the mirror of progress, the realization of success, a proof of one's condition, a revelation of the future, a sign of glory.
The structure he gives is simple and has been used ever since. Before everything else, he says, let us list sincere thanksgiving first on our prayer card. On the second line we should put confession and heartfelt contrition of soul. And then let us present our petition to the King of all. This is the best way of prayer, as it was shown to one of the brethren by an angel of the Lord. Thanksgiving, confession, petition, in that order, and the order is the instruction.
On the words themselves, John is emphatically for brevity, which is worth noting in a writer this fond of lists. Let your prayer be completely simple, he says, for both the publican and the prodigal son were reconciled to God by a single phrase. Do not be over-sophisticated in the words you use, because the simple and unadorned lisping of children has often won the heart of their heavenly Father. Do not attempt to talk much when you pray, in case your mind is distracted searching for words. One word of the publican propitiated God, and one cry of faith saved the thief. Loquacity in prayer often distracts the mind and leads to phantasy, whereas brevity makes for concentration.
He gives the three stages of prayer, and they are the standard sequence of the tradition. The beginning of prayer consists in banishing the thoughts that come to us by single ejaculations, the very moment they appear. The middle stage consists in confining our minds to what is being said and thought. And its perfection is rapture in the Lord.
He is precise about the four ways prayer goes wrong, and the distinctions are genuinely useful for anyone who has tried it. Soiled prayer is one thing, its disappearance is another, robbery is another, and defection is another. Prayer is soiled when we stand before God and picture irrelevant and inopportune thoughts. Prayer is lost when we are captured by useless cares. Prayer is stolen from us when our thoughts wander before we realize it. And prayer is spoilt by any kind of attack or interruption that comes to us at the time of prayer.
His instruction for the wandering mind is patient and mechanical, and it is the most encouraging thing in the chapter for a beginner. Try to lift up, or rather to shut off, your thought within the words of your prayer, he says, and if in its infant state it wearies and falls, lift it up again. Instability is natural to the mind, but God is powerful to establish everything. If you persevere indefatigably in this labour, then he who sets the bounds to the sea of the mind will visit you too, and during your prayer will say to the waves: thus far shall you come, and no further. Earlier in the book he had already said it more plainly: God does not require from those still under obedience a prayer completely free of distractions. Do not despond when your thoughts are filched, but remain calm and unceasingly recall your mind. Unbroken recollection is proper only to an angel.
He is realistic that quantity precedes quality. A great practiser of high and perfect prayer says, I would rather speak five words with my understanding, John notes, but such prayer is foreign to infant souls. Therefore, imperfect as we are, we need not only quality but a considerable amount of time for our prayer, because the latter paves the way for the former.
And he does not let the reader treat prayer as pleasant. A convict does not fear his sentence of punishment as much as a fervent man of prayer fears this duty of prayer, he says. Also this, which is a rule about interruption: it is cruel to snatch water from the mouth of a thirsty person, but it is crueller still for a soul that is praying with compunction to be torn away from its beloved task before it has finished. And: do not abandon prayer until you see that, by divine providence, the fire and the water have fallen off, because you may not have such a moment for the remission of your sins again in all your life.
Two diagnostics from this rung are worth keeping. First: your prayer will show you what condition you are in. Theologians say that prayer is the monk's mirror. Second, a test that is about behaviour after prayer rather than during it: when you have prayed soberly, you will soon be fighting against fits of temper, for this is what our enemies aim at. That gives a person something checkable. Prayer that is doing its work is followed by an attack on the temper, and the state of the temper afterwards is the report card.
He describes two kinds of experience at the end of prayer, and he does not treat either as failure. When the holy and heavenly fire comes to dwell in the souls of those who merely keep watch over the heart, he says, it burns them, because they still lack purification, whereas it enlightens those who supervise the heart by means of the mind, according to the degree of their perfection. For one and the same fire is called both the fire that consumes and the light that illuminates. That is why some people come away from prayer as if they were marching out of a furnace, and feel relief as from some defilement, while others come away as if illumined with light and clothed in a garment of joy and humility. But those who come from prayer having experienced neither of these two effects have prayed bodily and not spiritually.
And he closes the rung by insisting that this is the one subject on which he cannot help. Have all courage, he says, and you will have God for your teacher in prayer. Just as it is impossible to learn to see by word of mouth, because seeing depends on one's own natural sight, so it is impossible to realize the beauty of prayer from the teaching of others. Prayer has a Teacher all its own.
Step Twenty-Nine: Dispassion
The title John gives this rung is Concerning heaven on earth, or godlike dispassion and perfection, and the resurrection of the soul before the general resurrection. He begins by noting the absurdity of his position. Here we are, lying in the deepest pit of ignorance, in the dark passions of this body and in the shadow of death, having the temerity to begin to philosophize about heaven on earth.
His definitions: the firmament has the stars for its beauty, and dispassion has the virtues for its adornments, for by dispassion I mean nothing other than the interior heaven of the mind, which regards the tricks of the demons as mere toys. And: he is truly dispassionate who has made his flesh incorruptible, who has raised his mind above created things and subdued all his senses to it, and who keeps his soul in the presence of the Lord, ever reaching out to him even beyond his own strength.
Some say dispassion is the resurrection of the soul before the body, he reports; others, that it is the perfect knowledge of God, second only to the angels'. And he insists it admits of degrees: there is a dispassionate man, and there is one who is more dispassionate than the dispassionate. The one strongly hates what is evil; the other has an inexhaustible store of virtues.
The structure of the chapter is a series of paired extremes, in which John defines each summit by inverting the corresponding vice, and the effect is a compressed restatement of the whole book. If it is the height of gluttony to force oneself to eat when one has no appetite, then it is the height of temperance for a hungry man to overcome nature when nature is blameless. If it is extreme sensuality to rave over irrational and even inanimate creatures, then it is extreme purity to hold all persons in the same regard as inanimate things. If it is the height of cupidity to go on collecting and never be satisfied, then it is the height of poverty not to spare even one's own body. If it is called a sea of wrath for a person to be savage even when nobody is about, then it will be a sea of long-suffering to be as calm in the presence of your slanderer as in his absence. If it is the height of vainglory for a person, seeing nobody near to praise him, to put on affected behaviour, then it is a mark of its absence not to let your thought be beguiled in the presence of those who do praise you. If it is a sign of pride to be arrogant even in poor clothing, then it is a mark of saving humility to have humble thoughts in the middle of high undertakings and achievements.
And he gives the final mark of dispassion as a report of absence, which is a strange and precise thing to claim. I take it as a mark of holy dispassion, he says, to be able to say honestly: the evil one who dodges me, I have not known, nor how he came, nor why, nor how he went, but I am completely unaware of everything of this kind, because I am wholly united with God, and always will be. The person who has arrived is not the person who has won all the fights. It is the person who has stopped noticing that they are happening.
He is careful to say the state is not partial. A king's diadem is not composed of one stone, he says, and dispassion does not reach perfection if we neglect even one virtue, however ordinary.
And then the passage that keeps this rung from being a wall. Imagine dispassion as the celestial palace of the Heavenly King, John says, and the many mansions as the dwellings within that city, and the wall of this celestial Jerusalem as the forgiveness of sins. Let us run, brothers, let us run, to enter the bridal hall of this palace. If we are prevented by anything, by some burden or old habit, or by time itself, what a disaster. But let us at least occupy one of those mansions around the palace. And if we sink down and grow weak, let us make sure of being at least within the walls. For he who does not enter there before his end, or rather, does not scale the wall, will lie out in the desert of fiends and passions.
That is a real concession and it should be heard as one. The palace is dispassion, the outbuildings are lesser attainments, and the wall is simply the forgiveness of sins. John's floor, for a reader who cannot manage anything else, is: get inside the wall.
Step Thirty: Faith, Hope, Love
The last rung. And now, finally, after all that we have said, John begins, there remain these three that bind and secure the union of all: faith, hope, love. And the greatest of these is love, for God himself is so called.
His image for the three: I see the first as a ray, the second as a light, the third as a circle, and in all of them one radiance and one splendour. The first can make and create all things. The divine mercy surrounds the second and makes it immune to disappointment. The third does not fall, does not stop in its course, and allows no respite to the man who is wounded by its blessed rapture.
He immediately declares the subject beyond him. He who wishes to speak about divine love undertakes to speak about God, he says, and it is precarious to expatiate on God and may even be dangerous for the unwary. The angels know how to speak about love, and even they can only do it according to the degree of their own enlightenment. God is love, so he who wishes to define this tries with bleary eyes to measure the sand in the ocean.
What he does give is a set of pairings that make love intelligible by analogy, and he insists there is nothing wrong with the method. There is nothing wrong in representing desire, and fear, and care, and zeal, and service, and love for God in images borrowed from human life. Blessed is he who has obtained such love and yearning for God as an enraptured lover has for his beloved. Blessed is he who fears the Lord as much as men under trial fear the judge. Blessed is he who is as zealous as a well-disposed servant toward his master. Blessed is he who stands in prayer before the Lord as servants stand before a king. Blessed is he who unceasingly strives to please the Lord as others try to please men. And: even a mother does not cling to the babe at her breast as a son of love clings to the Lord at all times.
He describes the state as a kind of permanent occupation of attention. He who truly loves always keeps the face of his beloved in his imagination, and there embraces it tenderly. Such a man can get no relief from his strong desire even in sleep; even then he holds converse with the one he loves. One who was wounded with love said of himself, and John says he wonders at it: I sleep because nature requires this, but my heart is awake in the abundance of my love.
He gives the tests, and they are entirely horizontal. He who loves the Lord has first loved his brother, because the second is proof of the first. One who loves his neighbour can never tolerate slanderers, but runs from them as from fire. And: he who says he loves the Lord but is angry with his brother is like a man who dreams that he is running.
On hope, which gets less attention than it deserves, John is compact. The power of love is in hope, because by hope we await the reward of love. Hope is a wealth of hidden riches, a treasure of assurance of the treasure in store for us. It is a rest from labours, the door of love, the superannuation of despair, an image of what is absent. The failure of hope is the disappearance of love; toils are bound by it and labours depend on it. A monk of good hope is a slayer of despondency, and with this sword he routs it. And a note on where hope comes from that closes the loop with the rest of the book: experience of the Lord's gift engenders hope, and he who is without experience remains in doubt.
And then the ending, which is the most personal writing in the Ladder. John addresses love directly, and asks it a question. Tell us, fairest of virtues, where you feed your flock, where you rest at noon. Enlighten us, quench our thirst, guide us, take us by the hand, for we wish at last to soar to you. You rule over all. And now you have ravished my soul. I cannot contain your flame.
But I long to know, he says, how Jacob saw you fixed above the ladder. Satisfy my desire. Tell me: what are the means of such an ascent? What is the manner, what is the law that joins together the steps which your lover sets as an ascent in his heart? I thirst to know the number of those steps, and the time needed for the ascent. He who knows the struggle and the vision has told us of the guides, but he would not, or rather could not, enlighten us any further.
And the answer he receives, appearing as he says from heaven and speaking in the ear of his soul, is the last word of the book. Unless, beloved, you renounce your gross flesh, you cannot know my beauty. May this ladder teach you the spiritual combination of the virtues. On the top of it I have established myself, as my great initiate said: and now there remain faith, hope, love, these three, but the greatest of all is love.
So Jacob's ladder had someone standing at the top of it, and that someone is love. Which means, on John's account, that the thirty rungs are not a route to a state of perfection but a route to a person, and that the whole ascetic apparatus of the book, the fasting, the vigils, the tears, the obedience, the humiliations, the tomb-visiting, the interrogation of vices, is instrumental all the way down. He says as much in the brief exhortation he appends at the end, and it is the only place in the thirty rungs where he raises his voice. Ascend, brothers, ascend eagerly, and be resolved in your hearts to ascend. Run, he says. And he notes, because the number has been the design of the whole book from the first page, that Christ was baptized in the thirtieth year of his visible age, and so attained the thirtieth step on the spiritual ladder, since God is indeed love.
Closing Synthesis
Set the whole book down and four things about it stand out.
The first is the sequencing claim, which is what makes this a ladder rather than a list. John's contention is that spiritual change has an order, that the order is not arbitrary, and that most failure is a sequencing error rather than a failure of will. A man attacks his pride and leaves his stomach and his tongue alone. A man goes into solitude carrying a temper. A man takes up contemplation while still needing to be right in an argument. On John's diagnosis these are not people who tried hard and failed. They are people who tried the wrong rung. He is explicit that the ladder can be climbed only from where you are standing, that nobody ascends in a single stride, and that the first sign of health is not a spiritual experience but a growing self-condemnation.
The second is the reversal at the top. The first twenty-three rungs are largely about effort: leave, obey, weep, fast, keep watch, hold your tongue, do not answer back. And then from meekness onward the grammar changes, and John spends more and more time explaining that the things he is describing cannot be produced by the reader. Purity is a gift and the man who tries to conquer his nature by himself struggles in vain. Humility is a nameless grace whose teacher is God indwelling, not an angel, not a man, and not a book. Prayer has a Teacher all its own. Dispassion is called heaven on earth. This is not John losing his nerve at the summit. It is the design. The effort of the lower rungs is not what causes the summit; it is what removes the obstacles to receiving it, and John's whole account of ascetic labour is an account of clearing space.
The third is what the book does with failure, and it is the reason the Ladder has outlived the culture it was written for. John's severity is total and his despair is zero, and he holds the two together by way of a single distinction: it is the property of men to fall and to rise again as often as it happens, and it is the property of devils alone not to rise once they have fallen. Falling is not the diagnostic. Staying down is. He tells the reader not to be surprised at falling every day. He says the man who despairs is committing suicide. He says the abyss of mercy has no bottom, that a fall is often the occasion of humility for anyone willing to use it that way, that recovered simplicity outranks simplicity that was never lost, and that the mourners in the Prison who had fallen were more blessed than the monks who had never fallen and never mourned. If pride made some angels into demons, he says, then humility can make angels out of demons, so those who have fallen may take courage.
The fourth is the shape of the pride problem, which John treats as the master vice precisely because it feeds on progress. Vainglory arrives with a wealth of labours and despair arrives with a multitude of wounds. Every virtue has a vice entwined with it like a frog coming up in a bucket of well water: gluttony with hospitality, cunning with discernment, familiarity with humility, and vainglory behind all of them. The demons dig three pits, and the third one, the praise, only opens after the first two have been defeated. Which means, on John's account, that there is no point on the ladder at which a person becomes safe, and that the one thing pride confesses it cannot touch is sincere self-accusation, since that is the one act that cannot be performed for an audience.
What John does not offer is anything for a reader who wants the ascent without the terms. He does not think the passions can be managed, only displaced. He does not think a person can be his own director. He does not think the body is neutral. He does not think tears are optional, or that the remembrance of death is morbid, or that a life not measured against its own ending can be spent well. Those are his convictions, arrived at in a monastery on Mount Sinai in the seventh century, written down for monks at the request of an abbot, and he did not qualify them. Neither has this companion.
The Principles Most Worth Remembering
A final recitation. These are the sentences from the Ladder that carry the most weight, restated plainly.
It is the property of angels not to fall. It is the property of men to fall and to rise again as often as it happens. And it is the property of devils, and devils alone, not to rise once they have fallen.
No one climbs a ladder in a single stride. The rungs are ordered, and the order is not decoration.
There are three motives for leaving anything: fear, hope of reward, and love. Fear begins in fragrance and ends in smoke. Reward turns forever in the same place like a millstone. Only love catches fire from itself.
Fervour returns only through the same door by which it left. When you lose it, find out what took it, and go back for it there.
Virtues watered by vainglory are water-loving plants. Move them to soil where nobody is watching and they wither at once.
Test the physician before you submit to him. After you have submitted, stop judging him. Choose the one whose character matches your particular sickness, not the one with the most impressive gifts.
What we do not confess, we do fearlessly, as though in the dark. Confession is the bridle.
Do not confess your own sins as though they belonged to someone else. You cannot escape shame except by shame.
Mourning and gladness are not opposites. Genuine repentance contains joy woven into it like honey in a comb, and it is a spur, not a weight.
You have not forgiven a man because you pray for him, or exchange gifts with him, or invite him to your table. You have forgiven him when you hear that misfortune has struck him and you weep for him as for yourself.
Do not pay attention to the feelings of a man who speaks disparagingly about his neighbour. Say: stop, brother, I fall into graver sins every day, so how can I criticize him? That heals two people with one plaster.
Despondency does not make a man lazy in everything. It makes him energetic about everything except the thing he committed to, and it arrives disguised as a more useful task. Its firstborn is the urge to change location. Its antidotes are singing and working with your hands.
Do not command a beginner to live on bread and water. That is telling a child to go up the whole ladder in one stride. Reduce by stages, and feed the body enough of the right food that it stops arguing.
A thought arriving is not a sin. Conversing with it may or may not be. Consenting to it with delight is the point at which something has been decided.
It is impossible for anyone to conquer his own nature. Offer God the weakness of your nature, fully admitting your incapacity, and you will imperceptibly receive what you could not take.
The love of money begins as a pretext of almsgiving and ends in hatred of the poor. So long as a man is collecting, he is generous.
Insensibility has no single parent. Find out what caused it in you, and fight its mother, because she is not the same in every case.
It is not darkness and loneliness that give the demons power over us, but barrenness of soul.
However you throw the prickly pear, a spike stands upright. Vainglory beams on every activity, including the activity of avoiding it, which is why it cannot be handled by adjusting outward behaviour.
God often hides even the perfections we have obtained. A man's praise opens our eyes, and as soon as our eyes are opened, the treasure vanishes.
Be exalted only by such merits as you had before you were born. Your mind was given you and your body is not yours, so the set of achievements you can properly boast of is empty.
Men can cure the lustful, angels the malicious, but only God the proud.
Pride keeps company with patience under dishonour, with obedience, with freedom from anger, and with service of one's neighbour. It is not the enemy of the ascent. It is the passenger.
The one thing pride admits it cannot touch is sincere self-accusation before God, because that is the one act that cannot be performed for an audience.
Blasphemous thoughts that arrive at the worst possible moment are not yours. Do not wrestle with them, because you would be trying to hold lightning in your hands. Despise them and walk on.
Meekness is the light and humility is the sun, and it is impossible to look at the sun before you have been in that light.
Simplicity recovered after a fall outranks simplicity that was never lost.
The demons dig three pits: to stop the good from being done, to spoil the way it is done, and to praise you for having done it. The third only opens once you have escaped the first two.
Every virtue comes up out of the well with a vice entwined around it, and behind all of them follows vainglory.
There are virtues and there are mothers of virtues. Go after the mothers. The daughters have plenty of teachers.
To learn God's will: kill your own will first, pray with honest simplicity, then ask, and accept the answer even if it contradicts you and even if the person answering is not impressive. And to remain undecided for a long time is the mark of an unenlightened and ambitious soul.
A man sick in soul who attempts solitude is a man who has jumped off a ship and expects to reach shore on a plank. If withdrawal makes you angrier, more resentful, and less loving, you are doing it wrong.
Pray in this order: thanksgiving, then confession, then petition. Keep the words few. One word of the publican was enough.
